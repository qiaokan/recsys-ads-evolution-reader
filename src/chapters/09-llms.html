Large language models in recommendation and advertising (2022–2026)

In this chapter

  • Name the six jobs large language models (LLMs) do in recommendation and advertising, and say which ones run at request time.
  • Compute why an LLM cannot score every candidate on every request, and how caching and distillation get around it.
  • Describe the production pattern that dominates published results: label with a large model, serve a small one.
  • Trace the LLM-as-recommender line from prompting ChatGPT to YouTube's PLUM, a Gemini-derived model serving retrieval.
  • Explain the open mechanism-design problem of putting ads inside generated answers.
  • Terms are defined at first use here and collected in the glossary.

The gist. LLMs entered recommendation and advertising mostly around the serving path, not in it. Scoring hundreds of candidates per request with a billion-parameter language model costs tens of thousands of times more compute than a production ranker, so the published successes use LLMs where cost is paid once: to embed item text, to label training data that a small model then learns from, to rewrite rare queries, to generate ad images and text, and to review ads against policy. Two lines go further. Recommenders built from LLM weights now serve retrieval at YouTube, and LLM assistants such as Amazon's Rufus, Google's AI answers and ChatGPT have become surfaces where ads are shown, with pricing rules still being invented.

Six jobs, sorted by distance from the serving path

An LLM is a Transformer trained to predict the next token of text at very large scale; in recommendation and ads it does six distinct jobs, and the closer a job is to the per-request ranking loop, the rarer it is in production. “Serving path” here means the computation that runs for every request within its latency budget (tens to hundreds of milliseconds, chapter 5).

Why an LLM rarely scores candidates at request time

A forward pass of a Transformer costs about two floating-point operations per parameter per token, so an LLM ranker is thousands of times more expensive per candidate than a production ranking model. The rule of thumb \text{FLOPs}\approx2PT, with P parameters and T tokens processed, counts one multiply and one add per weight per token and ignores attention's smaller share for short inputs.

Worked example

Scoring 500 candidates with a 7-billion-parameter LLM

Given (illustrative): an LLM with P=7\times10^9 parameters; each candidate described in T=100 tokens; 500 candidates per request; 10,000 requests per second. For comparison, a deep ranking model costing 5\times10^7 FLOPs (50 megaFLOPs) per candidate, a plausible order of magnitude for an embedding-plus-MLP ranker.

  1. LLM cost per candidate: 2PT=2\times7\times10^9\times100=1.4\times10^{12} FLOPs.
  2. Per request: 500\times1.4\times10^{12}=7\times10^{14} FLOPs. The ranker: 500\times5\times10^7=2.5\times10^{10}. Ratio 2.8\times10^4.
  3. Per second at 10,000 requests: 7\times10^{18} FLOPs. At an assumed 10^{15} FLOPs per second per accelerator, the order of a current data-centre GPU's peak, that is about 7,000 accelerators at perfect utilization, for ranking alone.

Result: per-request LLM ranking costs about 28,000 times the ranker in this example. In practice the prompt also contains the user's history; encoding it once and reusing it across candidates keeps the total near this figure rather than adding to it. At a realistic 30–50% utilization the count is 15,000–25,000 accelerators.

Check: the cost scales linearly in all three of P, T and candidates. A 1-billion-parameter model reading 20-token item IDs instead of descriptions costs 2PT=2\times10^9\times20=4\times10^{10} FLOPs per candidate, still 800 times the ranker. That is why production systems that do use LLM weights at request time apply them in retrieval, once per request, rather than once per candidate.

Three techniques move LLM cost out of the request. Caching: run the LLM once per item or per frequent query and store the result (an embedding, a rewritten query, a label). Distillation: use LLM outputs as training labels for a small model that serves (below). Tail routing: answer frequent (“head”) queries from cache and send only rare ones to a live model; Instacart reports that only about 2% of its query traffic reaches real-time LLM inference Post.

Encoder: item text becomes vectors

The least controversial use is to embed item content with a pretrained language model, which gives new and rarely seen items a meaningful vector before they have any interactions. This attacks cold start, the oldest weakness of collaborative filtering (chapter 3).

  • Kuaishou LEARN (AAAI 2025): a frozen 7-billion-parameter language model (Baichuan2-7B) encodes item content, and a second, trainable two-tower stage aligns those vectors with user behaviour. Deployed in Kuaishou's short-video feed advertising since January 2024; in a 9-day A/B test on 20% of traffic (on a platform with more than 400 million daily active users), ad revenue from cold-start items rose 8.77% and from long-tail users 5.79% Paper.
  • Xiaohongshu NoteLLM (WWW 2024): LLaMA-2-7B compresses each user post into a single embedding token, trained contrastively (pulling embeddings of co-engaged posts together and pushing others apart) with hashtag and category generation as auxiliary tasks, for item-to-item recommendation. A week-long A/B test against the previous encoder, SentenceBERT (a smaller pretrained sentence-embedding model), reported click-through rate +16.20% Paper.
  • Semantic IDs from text: TIGER builds its item codes from a frozen text encoder (chapter 8); Spotify described semantic IDs shared between generative search and recommendation (RecSys 2025) Paper.

The catch is that text knows nothing about who engages with what. Two songs with similar descriptions can have entirely different audiences. Work such as CoLLM (IEEE TKDE 2025) maps collaborative-filtering embeddings into the LLM's token space through a small network, and LC-Rec (ICDE 2024) adds learned semantic-ID tokens to LLaMA's vocabulary with alignment tasks, so the model sees both content and behaviour Paper. The same tension, content generalizes while IDs memorize, appeared with semantic IDs in chapter 8.

Labeler and teacher: label with the big model, serve the small one

The production pattern with the most published evidence is to have an LLM judge relevance at a scale no human team could, then train a small, fast model on those judgments. The small model is the student, the LLM the teacher; the technique is knowledge distillation. It turns the LLM's cost into a one-time labeling bill.

Why this works: relevance labels are the expensive input to every ranker, and human labeling scales to thousands of judgments a day while an LLM scales to millions. The student inherits the teacher's judgment on the distribution it was trained on, at a cost per query of a small model. What can go wrong: the student also inherits the teacher's biases and blind spots; LLM labels track the prompt; and a model trained on LLM-judged relevance optimizes what the LLM thinks is relevant, which is a proxy for what users do, the same proxy problem as every objective in chapter 7.

A variant uses LLM output as features rather than labels. Huawei's KAR (RecSys 2024) has an LLM write out reasoning about each user's preferences and factual knowledge about items, encodes that text, and feeds it to existing click-through-rate models; online it reported +7% recall in a news app and +1.7% song plays in a music app Paper.

Query and intent understanding

LLMs rewrite the rare, oddly worded queries that keyword and embedding retrieval handle worst, and the gains concentrate there. Alibaba's BEQUE (WWW 2024 companion), live on Taobao search since October 2023, fine-tunes an LLM to rewrite long-tail queries and aligns it with offline feedback from the search engine itself. In a 14-day A/B test against the previous rewriter Paper (the segment rows count only queries that were actually rewritten):

The authors themselves set aside the merchandise-value changes for the small segments as too noisy. The pattern, large gains on a small slice and a small gain overall, is typical: head queries were already well served. Amazon's COSMO (SIGMOD 2024 companion) used an instruction-tuned LLM to build a commonsense knowledge graph of shopping intents (“shoes for a beach wedding” relates to sandals and light colours) across 18 product categories and deployed it in search navigation; the published offline results are large (for example +60% in macro-F1, an accuracy measure averaged over classes, with frozen encoders), while the paper's abstract describes the online A/B gains only as significant Paper.

The LLM as the recommender

Using the LLM itself to rank went from prompting a general chatbot, which underperformed trained recommenders, to fine-tuning, to building generative recommenders from LLM weights with a learned item vocabulary, the version that reached production.

  1. 2022
    P5 (RecSys): one text-to-text model (T5-style) trained on five recommendation task families phrased as prompts, with some transfer to unseen prompts Paper.
  2. 2023
    Prompting ChatGPT (Liu et al., CIKM 2023 workshop): reasonable at rating prediction, weak at sequential and direct recommendation, with explanations people found clear. LLMRank (Hou et al., ECIR 2024): promising zero-shot ranking, but the models ignored the order of a user's history and were biased by item popularity and by the position of candidates in the prompt; shuffling and re-ranking reduced this Paper.
  3. 2023
    TALLRec (RecSys): LoRA fine-tuning of LLaMA-7B on a yes/no “will this user like this item” task beat traditional models and prompted GPT-3.5 with fewer than 100 training examples per task, on movie and book data Paper.
  4. 2025
    LinkedIn 360Brew: a 150-billion-parameter decoder-only model handling more than 30 ranking tasks from a text description of the member and task, reported at offline parity with or better than production models, in pre-production; arXiv later withdrew the preprint for a licensing reason, not a retraction of results Paper.
  5. 2025
    YouTube PLUM (Google DeepMind and YouTube, October preprint): continued pretraining of a Gemini-1.5 mixture-of-experts model (more than 900 million parameters active per token, of about 4.2 billion in total) on YouTube data with semantic IDs, then generative retrieval. Reported in production for YouTube retrieval. In a live A/B test that added PLUM as a retrieval source, against giving the same candidate quota to the production retrieval model: Shorts click-through rate +4.96% and views +0.39%, long-form click-through rate +0.76% and views +0.80% Paper.
  6. 2025
    Kuaishou OneRec-Think (October preprint): interleaves item tokens with generated text reasoning, trained in stages ending with reinforcement learning; reasoning is computed ahead of the request to fit serving. Online app stay time +0.159% Paper.

What the LLM contributes in the production version is weights and world knowledge, not its text vocabulary. PLUM does not ask Gemini to name videos; it adapts Gemini's weights to read and emit semantic-ID tokens, so the model inherits language understanding (useful for titles, descriptions and searches) while learning YouTube's catalogue as a new vocabulary. That is the generative-recommender architecture of chapter 8 initialized from a language model. Meta, separately, said on its July 2025 earnings call that LLMs drive “a meaningful share” of recent ranking-related time-spent gains on Threads, its text-based social app Call.

Reading LLM-recommender papers

Most academic results use small public datasets (MovieLens, Amazon reviews) on which LLMs may have seen the items, and sometimes the reviews, during pretraining, and many evaluate against a small sampled candidate set, the practice criticized in chapter 5. Treat leaderboard gains from prompting as weak evidence; the production results above are company-measured A/B tests.

Advertiser tools: generating ads and reviewing them

Generative models now write and draw ads inside the automated campaign products of chapter 6, and LLMs review submitted ads against advertising policy.

  • Meta began rolling out background generation, image expansion and text variations to all advertisers in October 2023 Post. On the July 2025 earnings call it said nearly 2 million advertisers used its video-generation tools and that a meaningful percent of ad revenue came from campaigns using generative features Call.
  • Google brought Gemini models to Performance Max asset generation in February 2024 and launched a Gemini-powered conversational campaign builder in Google Ads in January 2024. It reported that advertisers using asset generation were 63% more likely to publish Performance Max campaigns rated “Good” or “Excellent” on Ad Strength, and small businesses using the conversational builder 42% more likely Post. These are observational comparisons (advertisers chose whether to use the tools), and Ad Strength is Google's own rating of a campaign's assets, not a measure of sales.
  • Policy enforcement. Google's 2023 Ads Safety Report said it had started applying Gemini to ads safety and used LLMs for nuanced policies such as financial-services scams; its 2024 report said it launched more than 50 enhancements to its LLMs for enforcement at scale Post. Review is a good fit: it runs once per ad, latency is minutes rather than milliseconds, and policies are written in language.

Conversational surfaces: ads inside answers

LLM assistants are becoming a place where shopping intent is expressed, and the major platforms have started selling ads there, even though the auction-over-slots model of thirty years does not apply.

  1. Mar 2023
    Microsoft says it is exploring ads in the Bing Chat experience, sharing ad revenue with publishers whose content informed the answers Post.
  2. Feb 2024
    Amazon Rufus, a shopping assistant, enters beta in the US app. Amazon began testing sponsored placements in Rufus in late 2024 (press reports). On its October 2025 earnings call Amazon said 250 million customers had used Rufus that year and that it was on track for more than $10 billion of incremental annualized sales Call.
  3. Oct 2024
    Google shows ads in AI Overviews on US mobile search Post; tests of ads in AI Mode, its conversational search, were announced in May 2025 (press reports).
  4. Nov 2024
    Perplexity launches sponsored follow-up questions in the US, stating that brands do not write or edit answers Post. Press reports in 2025–2026 describe it winding the program down.
  5. Jan 2026
    OpenAI announces ad tests in ChatGPT for logged-in US adults on its Free and Go tiers Post.

The mechanism-design problem: what exactly is being sold? In a position auction (chapter 3) the good is a slot with a known click rate. In a generated answer the good can be a placement beside the answer, a mention inside it, or influence over its wording. Three research proposals from 2024, by researchers at or working with Google, Paper:

  • Token auction (Dütting, Mirrokni, Paes Leme, Xu and Zuo, WWW 2024 best paper): each advertiser is represented by an LLM agent that proposes a probability distribution over the next token; an aggregation rule combines the proposals token by token, weighted by bids. If the aggregation is monotone (bidding more never moves the output away from your preferred text), second-price-style payments make truthful bidding optimal, under the paper's assumption about how agents rank possible outputs (it gives them preferences over distributions rather than explicit valuations).
  • Auctions with LLM summaries (Dubey et al., KDD 2024): run a factorized auction for which ads to include, then let an LLM write a summary featuring them, with a model predicting each ad's prominence in the summary linking the two, so payments can reflect prominence.
  • RAG ad auctions (Hajiaghayi, Lahaie, Rezaei and Shin, NeurIPS 2024): allocate ads to segments of an answer produced by retrieval-augmented generation (RAG: the model conditions on retrieved documents, here ads), with incentive-compatible pricing.

Inference. None of these is a published production mechanism, and the deployed products described above use placements next to the answer rather than paid influence on its wording. The business constraint is trust: an assistant whose answers are visibly bought loses the attention that made it valuable. Expect measurement to rely on incrementality tests (chapter 7), because a click on an ad beside an answer that already satisfied the user means less than a click on a search result.

Simulated users

LLM agents are also used as synthetic users to test recommenders before real users see them, a promising but unvalidated idea. Agent4Rec (SIGIR 2024 perspective paper) created 1,000 LLM agents with profiles, memory and action modules seeded from MovieLens ratings and let them browse a simulated recommender; the agents reproduced some real-user behaviour and diverged from it in other respects Paper. Simulators are cheap to run, but a policy tuned against a simulator learns the simulator's quirks, the same off-policy problem as in chapter 7 in a new form.

What is established, and what is not

Established by published company A/B tests

LLM embeddings help cold-start and long-tail items. LLM-labeled data distilled into small models improves search relevance at several companies. LLM query rewriting helps rare queries. An LLM-initialized generative model serves retrieval at YouTube. (Separately, earnings calls report wide advertiser adoption of generative creative tools.)

Not established

That prompting a general LLM beats a trained recommender. That LLMs can rank every candidate in a high-traffic system at acceptable cost. That generated ad creative raises advertiser sales, as opposed to platform-defined quality scores. How ads inside generated answers should be priced and measured.

Inference. The economics in the worked example decide most of this. Where the cost is paid once per item, per query or per ad, LLMs are already standard; where it would be paid once per candidate per request, they enter only through distillation or as the initialization of a model designed to be served, which is the direction PLUM and OneRec point.
