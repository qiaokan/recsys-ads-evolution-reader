Era V (2021–2026): scaling laws and generative recommenders

In this chapter

  • Explain why models in the style of Meta's Deep Learning Recommendation Model (DLRM, chapter 5) stopped improving with more parameters, and what changed to restore scaling.
  • Build a semantic ID with residual quantization and explain generative retrieval (Google's TIGER, Transformer Index for GEnerative Recommenders).
  • Describe the reformulation by Meta's HSTU (hierarchical sequential transduction units) of ranking and retrieval as sequential transduction, and its reported scale.
  • Separate what is published about 2024–2025 production systems (Kuaishou's OneRec, Meta's Andromeda retrieval engine and GEM (Generative Ads Model) ranking model, Netflix's foundation model) from what is only claimed.
  • Name the open problems for ads in conversational AI answers.
  • Terms are defined at first use here and collected in the glossary.

The gist. By the early 2020s, ranking models had grown mostly by adding embedding rows, which raised memory but not quality much. Two ideas changed the slope. First, treat a user's history as a long sequence of actions and train a Transformer-like model to predict the next action, as language models predict the next token; at Meta this produced quality that kept improving with compute across three orders of magnitude. Second, give items short token codes (semantic IDs) built from their content, so a model can generate the next item's code directly instead of searching for it. In 2025 Kuaishou reported an end-to-end generative pipeline in production, and Meta described large foundation ad models whose knowledge is transferred into serving models. Most of the evidence is from the companies themselves.

Did recommendation models scale? Not by adding embedding rows

Through about 2022 most growth in recommender parameter counts was embedding tables, and quality gains from more parameters were small; the compute spent per example stayed modest. Systems papers advertised trillions of parameters (Persia, from Kuaishou and ETH Zürich, 2021, trained up to 100 trillion), but almost all of them sit in sparse lookups, each example touching only a few. Ardalani et al. (Meta, 2022) studied scaling laws for DLRM-style models and found that growing data improved quality more efficiently than growing parameters, with parameter scaling showing diminishing returns. The interaction and multilayer-perceptron (MLP) layers, where compute per example is spent, were tiny by language-model standards.

The 2024 answer was to spend compute on interactions and sequences. Meta's Wukong (Zhang et al., ICML 2024) stacked factorization-machine-style interaction blocks and reported a scaling law across two orders of magnitude of model complexity, beyond 100 GFLOP (billion floating-point operations) per example, on internal data Paper. The larger departure was HSTU, below.

Freshness kept mattering

Scale did not remove the need for fresh models. ByteDance's Monolith (Liu et al., 2022) described the training system behind real-time recommendation: a collisionless embedding table based on cuckoo hashing (no hash collisions between ids), expiring embeddings for stale ids, frequency filtering for rare ones, and online training that pushes sparse parameters to serving every few minutes while dense parameters sync daily Paper. It answers the same freshness problem Facebook measured in 2014 (chapter 4).

Semantic IDs and generative retrieval

Generative retrieval replaces nearest-neighbour search with decoding: the model outputs a short code that identifies the next item, and codes are built so that similar items share prefixes. Google's TIGER (Rajput et al., NeurIPS 2023) builds a semantic ID for each item in two steps. A frozen, pretrained text encoder (Sentence-T5) embeds the item's metadata (title, brand, category); a learned encoder maps that embedding to a latent vector \mathbf z, which is then quantized level by level. Each level l has a codebook, a learned set of vectors \{\mathbf e^{(l)}_c\} indexed by c (256 of them in TIGER), and picks the one nearest to what remains. With m levels:

\mathbf r_0=\mathbf z,\qquad c_l=\arg\min_c\bigl\lVert\mathbf r_l-\mathbf e^{(l)}_c\bigr\rVert,\qquad \mathbf r_{l+1}=\mathbf r_l-\mathbf e^{(l)}_{c_l},\qquad l=0,\dots,m-1.

The item's ID is the tuple (c_0,c_1,\dots,c_{m-1}): c_0 encodes the coarse region of embedding space, each later code refines the residual. The encoder, codebooks and a decoder that reconstructs the text embedding from the codes are trained together as an RQ-VAE (residual-quantized variational autoencoder); TIGER used three levels of 256 codes plus an extra token to separate items that collide. A sequence-to-sequence Transformer reads the user's history as a sequence of semantic-ID tokens and decodes the next item's tokens with beam search.

Worked example

Why semantic IDs help cold start (items with no interactions yet) and memory

Given: three levels of 256 codes each.

  1. Addressable IDs: 256^3=16{,}777{,}216 distinct tuples.
  2. Output vocabulary the decoder needs: 3\times256=768 token embeddings (one table per level), instead of one row per item (1,024 counting TIGER's extra collision code).
  3. A new item gets a semantic ID from its content embedding immediately; if it lands next to popular items in content space it shares their prefix, so the model can generate it before it has any interactions.

Result: 16.8 million addressable items with 768 output embeddings, and an ID for a new item on day zero.

Check: the trade-off is the inverse. Two items with similar content but different audiences share a prefix and must be told apart by later codes, so semantic IDs alone memorize less than one free embedding per item. YouTube's ranking study (Singh et al., RecSys 2024) found semantic-ID features generalized better on new and long-tail items (the many items that each get few interactions) while matching the overall quality of a free embedding per item ID.

Earlier, P5 (Geng et al., RecSys 2022) had cast several recommendation tasks as text-to-text prompts for one language model, and zero-shot rankers built on large language models (LLMs), with no recommendation training, showed that general language models know something about item similarity. Neither ran at production latency; semantic IDs were the bridge that made generation over a catalogue tractable.

HSTU: recommendation as sequential transduction

Meta's HSTU (Zhai et al., ICML 2024) recast both retrieval and ranking as predicting the next element of a user's chronological stream of (item, action) events, and reported that quality improved as a power law of training compute over three orders of magnitude. The paper's title, “Actions speak louder than words”, states the premise: the data are not text but actions, and a recommendation model should be trained like a language model on them. Key elements:

  • Reformulation. Heterogeneous features (demographics, counters such as an item's recent view count, context) are folded into the sequence as tokens or dropped; the model reads interleaved items and actions. Retrieval predicts the next item; ranking predicts the action given the history and a candidate placed in the sequence. Called generative recommenders (GRs).
  • Layer. Each HSTU layer takes the matrix X of input event embeddings (one row per event) and computes U,V,Q,K=\operatorname{Split}(\phi_1(f_1(X))), then A=\phi_2(QK^{\top}+\text{rab}^{p,t}) and Y=f_2(\operatorname{Norm}(AV)\odot U), where f_1,f_2 are linear layers; Q,K,V are query, key and value matrices as in a Transformer; U is a gating matrix; \phi_1,\phi_2 are the SiLU function x\,\sigma(x); \text{rab}^{p,t} is a learned relative bias for position and time gap; \operatorname{Norm} is layer normalization; and \odot is the elementwise product. The attention is pointwise, not softmax-normalized, so the amount of relevant history is not normalized away; the same choice the Deep Interest Network (DIN, chapter 5) made in 2018 (Inference: the paper does not draw this link). The gating by U plays the role of the explicit feature crosses of chapter 5.
  • Efficiency. Stochastic sequence-length truncation during training and an inference algorithm (M-FALCON, microbatched fast attention leveraging cacheable operations) that scores many candidates in one pass by caching the shared history computation. The paper reports HSTU 5.3 to 15.2 times faster than FlashAttention2-based Transformers on 8,192-event sequences.
  • Reported scale and result Paper: models of 1.5 trillion parameters; a 12.4% improvement in online A/B tests on the paper's chosen metric; and a power-law fit of quality against training compute across three orders of magnitude, up to roughly GPT-3 / LLaMA-2 scale of training compute.

How strong is this evidence?

The architecture and offline results are public, and open-source implementations exist. The online gain and the production scaling curve are Meta's internal measurements against Meta's previous production system. That is the best evidence available for the scaling claim, but it is a single company's A/B test. The most significant claim is the scaling curve itself: if it holds elsewhere, recommendation quality becomes, like language modelling, mainly a matter of compute budget, which favours the largest platforms.

End-to-end generative pipelines: OneRec

Kuaishou's OneRec replaced the retrieve-then-rank cascade with a single generative model in production, the first public report of this at large scale. The February 2025 paper (“OneRec: Unifying retrieve and rank with generative recommender and preference alignment”, titled “…iterative preference alignment” on its arXiv listing) used an encoder–decoder with semantic IDs, a mixture-of-experts decoder (expert subnetworks selected per token, as in chapter 5's MMoE) that generates a whole session's list of videos, and a preference-alignment stage in which a reward model scores generated lists and the generator is tuned toward preferred ones (borrowing direct preference optimization from LLM alignment). The June 2025 technical report states Paper:

  • deployed in Kuaishou's main scenarios, handling about 25% of total queries per second;
  • stay time (total time users spend in the app) up 0.54% in the Kuaishou app and 1.24% in Kuaishou Lite;
  • operating cost about 10.6% of the traditional cascade's, and model FLOPs utilization (MFU; FLOPs are floating-point operations; the share of the accelerators' peak arithmetic throughput actually used) of 23.7% in training and 28.8% in inference, far above typical cascaded rankers, because one large dense model replaces many small, memory-bound ones.

Why the cost claim matters more than the engagement claim. A cascade runs dozens of models, most of them bottlenecked on memory lookups rather than arithmetic, so accelerators sit mostly idle. A single generative model is compute-bound and can use the same hardware and serving stack as LLMs. If the reported utilization generalizes, the economic case for generative recommendation is cheaper serving of a better model, not only better quality Inference.

Foundation models for ads and recommendations

In 2024–2025 the largest platforms described a common pattern: one or a few very large models trained on all user activity, whose knowledge is transferred into many smaller, task-specific serving models. Public descriptions, with their evidence grade:

The shared logic is economic. Serving latency caps the size of any model that scores every candidate, but training compute is not capped that way. A foundation model can be trained far beyond servable size and then used to produce embeddings, labels or soft targets for the servable models (knowledge distillation). This is the same split as Amazon's item table in 2003 (chapter 3), at a much larger scale: heavy work offline, light work at request time Inference.

Ads in AI answers: the slot disappears

Generative AI answers remove the ranked list of slots that thirty years of ad mechanisms assumed, and pricing and measurement for them are still being worked out. Google began showing ads within AI Overviews on US mobile search in October 2024 (desktop followed in 2025). OpenAI announced in January 2026 that it would test ads in ChatGPT for logged-in adult users in the US on its Free and Go tiers, with paid tiers remaining ad-free Post.

Three questions are open Inference:

  • Allocation. A position auction needs slots with click rates \alpha_j (chapter 3). An answer has at most one or two placements and an influence on the answer itself, so the mechanism must price “mention” and “placement next to the answer” rather than position. Proposals in the research literature include auctions over LLM-generated text that mix advertisers' preferred outputs; none is a public production standard.
  • Measurement. Click-through is a weak signal when the answer already satisfies the user; incrementality tests (chapter 7) become the only credible measure of value, and they are expensive.
  • Trust. The answer's credibility is the product. Any ad that changes the answer, rather than appearing beside it, risks the core asset, which constrains ad load (the share of the answer given to ads) in a way that a feed of videos does not.

Pre-mortem: how could Era V disappoint?

Assume that by 2028 generative recommenders have not displaced cascades outside a few companies; the likely causes are already visible.

  • The scaling curve does not transfer. It was measured on one company's data and metric; smaller platforms have less data per user, and compute-optimal scaling may require data they do not have.
  • Serving cost meets advertising margins. Generating lists with beam search over long histories per request is expensive; the OneRec cost claim depends on replacing an inefficient cascade, which not every company runs.
  • Control. Cascades expose levers (filters, boosts that multiply some items' scores, diversity rules, ad load) that product, policy and legal teams rely on; an end-to-end generator makes these harder to enforce and audit.
  • Measurement lag. Gains reported as attributed or modelled conversions (chapter 7) may not survive incrementality tests (exercise D8).

Even so, the direction from 1992 to 2026 has been consistent: each era replaced hand-designed structure with learned structure once data and compute allowed. Generative recommenders are the most extreme version yet, removing even the hand-designed boundary between retrieval and ranking.
