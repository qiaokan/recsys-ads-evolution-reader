The ads track (2007–2026): exchanges, first-price auctions and autobidding

In this chapter

  • Trace how pricing moved from the advertiser's cost-per-click (CPC) bid to the platform bidding on the advertiser's behalf for conversions (the actions the advertiser wants, such as purchases or installs).
  • Derive the budget-paced bid v/(1+\mu) in a second-price auction, where v is the impression's value and \mu the budget's shadow price, and the update rule that learns \mu.
  • Explain why display advertising moved to first-price auctions in 2017–2019, and derive optimal bid shading against a known competing-bid distribution.
  • Derive why an autobidder with a target cost per acquisition (CPA) bids a uniform multiple of value, sometimes above value.
  • Explain how platforms predict an install's seven-day (D7) revenue for target-ROAS bidding, why the loss function decides the bid, and what goes wrong in production.
  • Name what advertisers gave up in exchange for campaign automation (Google Performance Max, Meta Advantage+).
  • Terms are defined at first use here and collected in the glossary.

The gist. In 2005 an advertiser chose keywords and typed a bid per click. By 2025 the advertiser typically states a budget and a target cost per conversion or return on ad spend, uploads creative assets (the images, videos and text from which ads are assembled), and the platform's models choose the audience, the placement (which app, site or slot), the creative (which asset to show) and the bid for each impression, one display of an ad. Three changes drove this: real-time exchanges that priced each impression separately; prediction good enough that the platform could take on conversion risk (optimized cost-per-mille and cost-per-click pricing, oCPM and oCPC); and a move from second-price auctions (the winner pays the highest competing bid) to first-price auctions (the winner pays its own bid) that made manual bidding even harder. The algorithms are constrained optimization: each bid is predicted value scaled by a multiplier that enforces the advertiser's budget or target.

Exchanges and real-time bidding: price every impression

From about 2009, display inventory (banner and video ad slots on websites and apps, as opposed to search ads) was increasingly sold impression by impression in real-time auctions, which turned buying into a per-impression prediction problem for the buyer. An impression is one display of one ad. Ad exchanges (Right Media, bought by Yahoo in 2007; DoubleClick's ad exchange AdX, which Google acquired with DoubleClick in 2008) let publishers, the owners of the sites and apps with ad slots, offer each impression to many buyers. Demand-side platforms (DSPs) bid on the advertiser's behalf, typically within about 100 milliseconds. The OpenRTB (open real-time bidding) project (started in late 2010; version 2.0 adopted by the Interactive Advertising Bureau, IAB, in January 2012) standardized the bid request and response (sources). A DSP's bid for an impression is its estimate of the advertiser's value: for a goal stated as a target cost per acquisition (CPA, dollars per conversion), predicted conversion probability times the target CPA. Most exchanges initially ran second-price auctions, under which bidding true value is the textbook advice.

Per-impression buying created new modelling problems for the buyer. The buyer must predict the probability of a conversion, the distribution of the highest competing bid (the bid landscape), and how quickly its budget will be spent. Zhang, Yuan and Wang (KDD 2014) derived optimal bidding functions under a budget given a winning-probability curve and found them concave in predicted value rather than linear, a consequence of the budget constraint combined with a concave winning-probability curve.

The platform takes on conversion risk: oCPM and oCPC

Once conversion prediction was reliable, platforms let advertisers bid for conversions while still allocating impressions by expected value, and the auction score became bid × pCTR × pCVR. Here pCTR is the predicted click-through rate (clicks per impression) and pCVR the predicted conversion rate (conversions per click). Under optimized CPM (oCPM; CPM, cost per mille, is a price per 1,000 impressions; Facebook, from 2012), the advertiser states a value per conversion; the platform converts it into an impression bid with its own models and charges per impression. Under optimized CPC (oCPC; Alibaba, Zhu et al., KDD 2017), the advertiser sets a cost-per-click bid and the system adjusts it up or down within a bounded range according to predicted conversion rate. In both, the ranking score for an ad with conversion target a (dollars per conversion) is

\text{eCPM}=1000\times a\times\text{pCTR}\times\text{pCVR}\quad(+\ \text{quality terms}),

where eCPM is the expected revenue per 1,000 impressions, the common currency in which ads with different pricing are ranked. Meta's public description of its auction ranks by “total value”: the advertiser's bid times the estimated action rate, plus an ad-quality term, so a lower bid with a better predicted response can win. Errors in pCTR or pCVR now move money directly: an inflated pCVR overbids on the advertiser's behalf. This is why conversion models such as the Entire Space Multi-task Model (ESMM, chapter 5) and the delayed-feedback corrections of chapter 7 were built by ad teams.

Budgets and pacing: one multiplier per campaign

Pacing is controlling how fast a campaign spends its budget over the day, so it neither runs out by mid-morning nor ends the day with money unspent.

Under a daily budget, the optimal bid in a truthful auction is value divided by 1+\mu, where \mu is the budget constraint's shadow price; pacing algorithms estimate \mu online. Suppose a campaign faces impressions i=1,\dots,n with values v_i and second-price costs p_i, and chooses which to win, x_i\in\{0,1\}, to maximize surplus subject to spending at most B:

Derivation

Budget-paced bidding

  1. Lagrangian with multiplier \mu\ge0: \sum_i x_i(v_i-p_i)-\mu\Bigl(\sum_ix_ip_i-B\Bigr)=\sum_ix_i\bigl(v_i-(1+\mu)p_i\bigr)+\mu B.
  2. For fixed \mu, win impression i exactly when p_i\le v_i/(1+\mu).
  3. In a second-price auction you pay p_i, the highest competing bid, and win when your bid exceeds it, so bidding b_i=v_i/(1+\mu) implements step 2.
  4. Choose \mu so that expected spend equals B if the budget binds; \mu=0 otherwise.

Result: one scalar per campaign scales all bids down proportionally.

Scope: this is exact for the linear relaxation, where fractions of impressions can be bought; with indivisible impressions it is optimal up to one marginal impression (exercise D4 leaves $2 unspent). Check: with an unlimited budget, \mu=0 and the bid is the value, the textbook second-price answer. As the budget shrinks, \mu rises and only the impressions with the highest value-to-price ratio are bought.

Online, \mu is updated by a dual gradient step after each of the day's T periods (say, minutes), with step size \eta>0: \mu\leftarrow\max\{0,\ \mu+\eta\,(\text{spend}_t-B/T)\}, raising it when spend runs ahead of the per-period target. Balseiro and Gur (Management Science, 2019) analyse this adaptive pacing and prove regret guarantees. Production pacing also uses throttling, entering only a random fraction of auctions (LinkedIn, Agarwal et al., KDD 2014; Yahoo's smart pacing, Xu et al., KDD 2015), which is simpler but discards the value ranking that the pacing multiplier preserves.

Display moves to first price: why, and how to shade

Between 2017 and 2019 most of programmatic display (display ads bought through automated auctions) moved from second-price to first-price auctions, because chains of auctions had made “second price” opaque and manipulable. Header bidding (from about 2014–2015, with the open-source Prebid.js wrapper) let publishers solicit bids from several exchanges at once before calling their ad server (the system that finally picks which ad fills the slot), so an impression passed through several auctions in sequence. Second-price rules lose their meaning in a chain: exchanges used undisclosed floors (minimum prices, also called reserve prices), and the price paid depended on which auction cleared it. Exchanges moved to first price, where the rule is simple (pay your bid); Google Ad Manager announced its move to a unified first-price auction in March 2019 and completed it that September. Buyers responded with bid shading, bidding below value by an amount estimated from the competition.

Against a known distribution of competing bids, the optimal first-price bid balances a higher margin against a lower win rate. Let the highest competing bid Z have CDF F and density f. Expected surplus is U(b)=(v-b)F(b); setting U'(b)=0 gives

v-b^*=\frac{F(b^*)}{f(b^*)}.

With Z\sim\text{Uniform}(0,L), F(b)/f(b)=b, so b^*=v/2.

In practice F is unknown and depends on the impression; shading models estimate it from win/loss logs, which are censored (a loss reveals only that the winning price exceeded your bid, and some exchanges report the minimum bid needed to win). This is a survival-analysis problem, and an example of how a market-design change creates a new prediction task.

Autobidding: the platform bids to the advertiser's target

Target-CPA and target-ROAS bidding (ROAS, return on ad spend, is attributed revenue divided by ad spend) make the platform maximize conversions, or conversion value (the revenue the advertiser reports for each conversion), subject to the advertiser's cost constraint, and in a truthful auction the optimal policy is to bid a uniform multiple of each impression's value. Such an autobidder is a value maximizer: it maximizes the total value won subject to spending constraints and, unlike a profit maximizer, gives no weight to the surplus (value minus price) on each impression. Google's Smart Bidding, Meta's cost-cap and value-optimization bidding and similar products all fit one model, analysed by Aggarwal, Badanidiyuru and Mehta (WINE 2019). Let v_i be the value of impression i in dollars (predicted conversions times target CPA, or predicted conversion value divided by target ROAS):

Derivation

Uniform bid scaling for a value maximizer

Problem: maximize \sum_ix_iv_i subject to the target \sum_ix_ip_i\le\sum_ix_iv_i (average cost per value at most 1) and the budget \sum_ix_ip_i\le B.

  1. Lagrangian with \lambda\ge0 for the target and \mu\ge0 for the budget: \sum_ix_i\bigl[(1+\lambda)v_i-(\lambda+\mu)p_i\bigr]+\mu B.
  2. Win impression i exactly when p_i\le\kappa v_i with \kappa=\dfrac{1+\lambda}{\lambda+\mu}.
  3. In a truthful auction that is implemented by bidding b_i=\kappa v_i, one multiplier for the whole campaign.
  4. If only the target binds (\mu=0), \kappa=(1+\lambda)/\lambda>1: the bidder bids above value on every impression.

Result: autobidding is value prediction times one learned multiplier; with a target and slack budget, the multiplier exceeds one. As with pacing, exact for the linear relaxation.

Check: overbidding is consistent with the target because the constraint is on the average. Cheap impressions bought far below value create slack that pays for expensive ones bought above value. A profit maximizer (surplus objective, as in the pacing derivation) never bids above value; a value maximizer with an average-cost target does.

The same paper shows that when all bidders are such value maximizers, the welfare of the Vickrey–Clarke–Groves (VCG) auction at equilibrium, measured as “liquid welfare” (total value won, with each advertiser's value capped by what it can spend), is at least half the best achievable. The worst-case ratio of optimal to equilibrium welfare is called the price of anarchy; here it is 2. This is a named result, not proved here. The practical consequence: as autobidders came to dominate, auction design research shifted from profit-maximizing bidders to value maximizers with return-on-spend constraints (limits on spend per unit of value, such as a target CPA or ROAS), and reserve prices and boosts (score multipliers the platform applies to some bidders) were re-derived for that model.

Predicting the value: D7 revenue and lifetime value

A target-ROAS bid needs a dollar value per impression before the user has done anything, so for app campaigns the platform predicts revenue over a fixed window after install, most often seven days (D7). That revenue is zero for most users and heavy-tailed for the few who pay, so production models predict P(pays) × E[revenue | pays], and their aggregate calibration matters more than how well they rank users. Lifetime value (LTV) is the revenue a user brings over a horizon; a predicted lifetime value (pLTV) is a model's estimate of it. In mobile apps the horizon is usually written as a day count: D0 and D7 revenue are the revenue in the first day and the first seven days. Platforms define the window differently. AppLovin counts purchases within 24 hours (D0) or 192 hours (D7) of the ad click Post. TikTok's Day 7 ROAS for ad-revenue optimization counts revenue in the first 7 days after install Post. Revenue comes from in-app purchases (IAP) and from in-app advertising (IAA): ads the app itself shows, whose per-user revenue is estimated from impression-level reports of the app's ad-mediation software. A whale is one of the few users who account for a large share of purchase revenue.

For a campaign optimized on installs, the platform's value for impression i is the install probability times the expected D7 revenue given install, divided by the advertiser's ROAS target \tau (written as a ratio, so 150% is \tau=1.5):

v_i=\text{pCTR}_i\times\text{pCVR}_i\times\frac{\mathbb E[R_7\mid\text{install},x_i]}{\tau},

where R_7\ge0 is the user's D7 revenue and x_i the impression's features; pCVR here is the predicted install rate per click. The bid is then \kappa v_i with the campaign multiplier \kappa derived above. Because the bid is linear in the predicted mean, an error of 20% in \mathbb E[R_7] is a 20% overbid or underbid on every impression, which is why the choice of loss function is a money decision.

Worked example

Why the loss decides the bid

Model one install segment as zero-inflated lognormal (ZILN, Wang, Liu and Miao at Google, 2019): with probability 1-p the user pays nothing; with probability p the user pays R_7 with \log R_7\sim\mathcal N(\mu,\sigma^2). Take p=0.03, \mu=3, \sigma=1.5 (illustrative values) and a ROAS target \tau=1.5.

  1. Median payer spend: e^{\mu}=e^{3}=\$20.09. Mean payer spend: e^{\mu+\sigma^2/2}=e^{4.125}=\$61.87, about three times the median because of the tail.
  2. Expected D7 revenue per install: p\,e^{\mu+\sigma^2/2}=0.03\times61.87=\$1.86. At \tau=1.5 the campaign can afford 1.86/1.5=\$1.24 per install.
  3. A payer model trained with squared error on \log R_7 learns \mathbb E[\log R_7]=\mu and predicts e^{\mu}, the median. It values the install at 0.03\times20.09=\$0.60 and bids \$0.40: low by the factor e^{\sigma^2/2}=3.08 (Jensen's inequality). Squared error on raw revenue has the right target but a gradient dominated by the few largest payers, so its estimates are noisy.
  4. ZILN fits all three parameters by maximum likelihood. The network outputs p (sigmoid), \mu (identity) and \sigma (softplus, to keep it positive), and the loss for a user with revenue y is -\mathbb 1\{y=0\}\log(1-p)-\mathbb 1\{y>0\}\Bigl[\log p-\log\bigl(y\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}\bigr)-\frac{(\log y-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\Bigr], binary cross-entropy on paying plus the lognormal negative log-likelihood on payers. The prediction is p\,e^{\mu+\sigma^2/2}.
  5. Sensitivity: if the model estimates \sigma=1.6 instead of 1.5, the predicted mean becomes 0.03\,e^{3+1.28}=\$2.17, 17% high (e^{0.155}=1.17), and every bid in the segment is 17% high.

Result: the bid needs the mean, the mean of a heavy tail depends exponentially on its spread, and a log-scale regression silently returns the median instead.

Check: with \sigma\to0 the median and mean coincide and all three losses agree. The larger the spread of payer revenue, the larger both the log-regression bias e^{\sigma^2/2} and the sensitivity to errors in \sigma, which is why later models stop assuming one lognormal.

Evaluation follows the same split between ranking and level. The normalized Gini coefficient (recommended for LTV in the ZILN paper) measures ranking: sort users by prediction, plot the cumulative share of actual revenue against the cumulative share of users (a Lorenz curve), and divide the model's Gini coefficient by that of the perfect ordering. Level is checked with decile charts, mean prediction against mean outcome in each tenth of users. For bidding, the decisive check is aggregate bias, total predicted against total realized revenue per campaign, country and platform, because a well-ranked but biased model misprices every impression.

The model is the easy part; five production problems decide whether it works.

  • Label delay. A D7 label exists only seven or more days after the click, so the model always trains on users acquired a week ago, under older creatives, prices and in-game events. Multi-horizon models help because their short-horizon heads (D0, D1) train on fresh labels and share layers with the D7 head; the alternatives are the delayed-feedback corrections of chapter 7.
  • Whale variance. Per-user errors are mostly noise. One whale can move a small campaign's measured ROAS by a large factor, so evaluate calibration on cohorts, not on users.
  • Selection bias. The platform observes revenue only for installs it won. Segments it bid low on produce few labels, so their whales are never learned; the remedy is deliberate exploration, bidding on some traffic the model undervalues.
  • iOS. Under SKAdNetwork 4, postbacks cover days 0–2, 3–7 and 8–35 after install, and only the first can carry the fine-grained 6-bit conversion value; the window ending on day 7 carries a coarse low, medium or high value Post. On iOS a “D7 value” is therefore usually the advertiser's prediction from the first two days, encoded into the conversion value.
  • The horizon is not the goal. D7 favours users who pay early. For subscription apps and games whose players take weeks to start paying, a campaign can meet its D7 target and lose money at day 180. For such apps, AppLovin's reporting also offers 14- and 28-day windows.

Inference. The advertiser-side model is the easier one: once a user has made a first purchase, D0 revenue is a strong predictor of D7, and the hard cases are users who have not paid yet and early detection of whales. That is also where the incentives bite. When the advertiser sends its own pLTV back as the conversion value, any upward bias becomes a higher bid, paid for by the advertiser. A team doing this should track predicted against realized D7 revenue by cohort and stop sending the prediction when the ratio drifts.

Campaign automation: the platform chooses almost everything

From 2021 the largest platforms bundled targeting, placement, creative selection and bidding into single automated products, so the advertiser's inputs shrank to a goal, a budget, creative assets and a conversion feed (the stream of conversions the advertiser reports back to the platform). Google's Performance Max (generally available in November 2021) runs one campaign across Search, YouTube, Display, Discover, Gmail and Maps; Meta's Advantage+ shopping campaigns (August 2022) automate audience and placement; AppLovin's AXON 2.0 (second quarter of 2023) drove a large rise in AppLovin's reported advertising revenue on mobile-game inventory (sources). Generative tools that produce or vary ad images and text were added to these products from 2023.

Why automation won

The platform sees cross-advertiser data the advertiser cannot: competing bids, user responses across campaigns, inventory forecasts. After Apple's App Tracking Transparency (ATT, chapter 7) removed much of the user-level signal advertisers used for their own targeting, broad automated targeting driven by the platform's models often performed as well or better.

What the advertiser gave up

Control and visibility: placement-level reporting, keyword control and exclusion levers were reduced. The platform both sets the bid and grades the result with its own attribution (its rules for crediting conversions to ads), a principal–agent problem. Advertisers' only independent check is a randomized incrementality test, which compares conversions between users shown and not shown the ads (chapter 7), and the platform often runs that too.

Inference. The steady direction is that the advertiser specifies an objective and the platform owns the optimization. That raises the value of the platform's predictive models and lowers the value of advertiser-side tooling; it also concentrates measurement in the party that is paid. The open question for 2026 is how ads are priced and measured in conversational AI answers, where there is no ranked list of slots to auction (chapter 8).
