Era III (2013–2020): embeddings, deep ranking and the multi-stage funnel

In this chapter

  • Draw the retrieval → ranking → re-ranking funnel and say why it exists.
  • Derive why YouTube's watch-time-weighted logistic regression predicts expected watch time.
  • Compare the explicit-interaction architectures (Wide & Deep, DeepFM, DCN, DLRM) by what they cross and at what cost.
  • Explain DIN's candidate-aware attention, two-tower retrieval with the logQ correction, and ESMM's fix for conversion-rate selection bias.
  • Summarize the reproducibility audits that deflated many neural gains.

The gist. Deep learning entered recommendation through embeddings: every id, word and category became a learned dense vector, and neural networks learned the interactions engineers used to hand-code. Because no network can score billions of items per request, systems split into a funnel: a cheap retrieval stage (two towers whose dot product can be searched with approximate nearest neighbours) narrows millions of items to thousands, and an expensive ranking network orders those. Ranking models grew explicit cross layers, attention over each user's behaviour history and multiple task heads. A wave of reproducibility studies then showed that many published neural gains came from weak baselines, a corrective worth carrying into Era V.

The funnel: spend compute where the decision is close

Every large recommender since about 2016 is a cascade of increasingly expensive models over decreasing candidate sets, because exact scoring of the whole corpus with the best model is unaffordable within the latency budget.

The design consequence is that each stage optimizes a different proxy. Retrieval is judged by recall of what the ranker would have chosen; ranking by calibrated, accurate scores on a biased candidate set; re-ranking by list-level objectives. Mismatch between stages (retrieval discarding items the ranker would love) is a persistent source of loss that Era V's generative models try to remove.

Embeddings: ids become vectors

The first deep idea to stick was representing each discrete entity as a learned vector, trained so that entities appearing in similar contexts get similar vectors. Microsoft's DSSM (Huang et al., CIKM 2013) mapped queries and documents through separate networks into a shared space and trained on clicks with a softmax over one clicked document and a few random ones; this is the two-tower architecture in its first form. After word2vec (Mikolov et al., 2013), the skip-gram objective was applied to sequences of items instead of words: purchase sequences in e-mail receipts (prod2vec, Grbovic et al., KDD 2015), item co-occurrence (item2vec, 2016), and booking sessions at Airbnb (Grbovic and Cheng, KDD 2018). Embeddings made similarity search over items cheap and transferable across tasks.

YouTube 2016: the template for deep recommendation

Covington, Adams and Sargin (RecSys 2016) set the template: a deep candidate generator trained as extreme multiclass classification, and a deep ranker trained to predict watch time.

  • Candidate generation treats “which video will this user watch next” as a softmax over millions of classes, P(w_t=i\mid U,C)=e^{\mathbf v_i^{\top}\mathbf u}/\sum_j e^{\mathbf v_j^{\top}\mathbf u}, where \mathbf u is the output of a network over averaged watch and search embeddings, demographics and context. Training uses sampled softmax; serving becomes a nearest-neighbour search for the largest \mathbf v_i^{\top}\mathbf u.
  • Freshness: an “example age” feature lets the model represent how popularity depends on time since upload; at serving it is set to zero (or slightly negative), so the model does not systematically favour old content.
  • Label design: predict the next watch rather than a randomly held-out one, because viewing is sequential (episode 2 follows episode 1).
  • Ranking uses logistic regression in which each clicked impression is weighted by its watch time T_i and each unclicked impression has weight 1.
Derivation

Why the weighted odds estimate expected watch time

Given: N impressions in some feature cell, k of them clicked with watch times T_1,\dots,T_k; click probability P=k/N; expected watch time per impression \mathbb E[T]=\sum_iT_i/N.

  1. A logistic regression fitted to weighted data learns odds equal to (weighted positives)/(weighted negatives) in the cell: \text{odds}=e^{\mathbf w^{\top}\mathbf x+b}=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^kT_i}{N-k}.
  2. Divide numerator and denominator by N: \text{odds}=\frac{\mathbb E[T]}{1-P}\approx\mathbb E[T]\,(1+P)\approx\mathbb E[T]\quad\text{for small }P.

Result: serving e^{\mathbf w^{\top}\mathbf x+b} instead of the sigmoid ranks by approximately expected watch time.

Check: with N=100, k=5, total watch 300 s: \mathbb E[T]=3 s, odds =300/95=3.16 s =3/(1-0.05).

Optimizing watch time instead of clicks was a deliberate objective change (YouTube had announced in March 2012 that related and recommended videos would weight time watched more heavily) aimed at click-bait thumbnails. It also illustrates the next problem: any single engagement proxy can be gamed, which led to multi-task objectives below and to the long-term-value work in chapter 7.

Learned feature interactions: from Wide & Deep to DLRM

The ranking-model literature of 2016–2021 is a search for architectures that learn feature crosses an engineer would otherwise hand-code, at acceptable serving cost. Inputs are dozens to hundreds of categorical fields, each mapped to an embedding \mathbf e_f\in\mathbb R^d, plus dense features.

Why explicit crosses at all? An MLP can approximate multiplication but learns it inefficiently from sparse data; Rendle et al. (2020) showed that an MLP struggles even to learn a dot product. Each DCN layer raises the polynomial degree of the represented crosses by one, so L layers represent crosses of degree up to L+1, at a parameter cost linear in d for DCN and quadratic (or low-rank) for DCN-V2.

The systems fact that shaped all of these: embeddings dominate. A model with hundreds of millions of ids per field has embedding tables of hundreds of gigabytes to terabytes while the dense layers are small. Training therefore moved from CPU parameter servers (Li et al., OSDI 2014) to GPU systems that shard embedding tables across devices and exchange looked-up rows (all-to-all) while replicating the MLPs; DLRM's paper describes exactly this hybrid parallelism, later packaged in libraries such as NVIDIA HugeCTR and Meta's TorchRec.

Behaviour sequences: attention keyed on the candidate

Alibaba's DIN (Zhou et al., KDD 2018) replaced the average of a user's past-behaviour embeddings with a weighted sum whose weights depend on the candidate ad, because a user's history contains many unrelated interests. For candidate ad embedding \mathbf v_A and history embeddings \mathbf e_1,\dots,\mathbf e_H:

\mathbf v_U(A)=\sum_{j=1}^{H}a(\mathbf e_j,\mathbf v_A)\,\mathbf e_j,

where a(\cdot) is a small feed-forward network. The weights are deliberately not softmax-normalized, so a user with many relevant behaviours produces a larger vector than one with a single weak match. Someone who bought a swimsuit and a laptop bag is represented differently when the candidate is goggles than when it is a mouse. The line continued with DIEN (AAAI 2019: a GRU models how interests evolve) and SIM (CIKM 2020: first retrieve the relevant subset of a very long, lifelong behaviour sequence, then attend over that subset), which pushed usable history from hundreds of events to tens of thousands (up to about 54,000).

In parallel, session-based recommenders borrowed language-model architectures. GRU4Rec (Hidasi et al., ICLR 2016) predicted the next click in a session with a recurrent network; SASRec (Kang and McAuley, ICDM 2018) used causal self-attention; BERT4Rec (Sun et al., CIKM 2019) used a bidirectional masked objective. Replication then complicated the ranking. Petrov and Macdonald (RecSys 2022) found published BERT4Rec-versus-SASRec comparisons inconsistent: BERT4Rec needs up to 30 times longer training, many later papers used an under-trained version, and properly trained it does beat SASRec. Klenitskiy and Vasilev (RecSys 2023) then showed that SASRec trained with a full-softmax cross-entropy loss, instead of its original sampled binary loss, outperforms BERT4Rec. Loss and training budget mattered as much as architecture. Sequence modelling returned at a far larger scale in Era V.

Two-tower retrieval and the logQ correction

Retrieval models score a user and an item with separate networks and a dot product, so item vectors can be precomputed and searched with approximate nearest neighbours; training them with in-batch negatives requires a correction for popularity. Write s(x,y)=\mathbf u(x)^{\top}\mathbf v(y). The ideal loss is a softmax over the whole corpus, which is unaffordable. Using the other items in a training batch as negatives is cheap, but items appear in batches in proportion to their frequency p_j, so popular items are over-represented as negatives and get pushed down too far. Yi et al. (RecSys 2019, YouTube) subtract the log sampling probability from each logit during training:

s^c(x,y_j)=s(x,y_j)-\log p_j,\qquad \mathcal L=-\log\frac{e^{s^c(x,y_i)}}{\sum_{j\in\text{batch}}e^{s^c(x,y_j)}}.

This is the standard importance-sampling correction for sampled softmax: dividing each sampled term by its proposal probability makes the batch sum an estimate of the full-corpus normalizer. The paper estimates p_j on the fly from a stream of item ids by tracking the average gap between successive occurrences of each item. At serving time the raw score s(x,y) is used.

Approximate nearest-neighbour search made retrieval over billions of vectors practical. Product quantization (Jégou, Douze and Schmid, 2011) compresses vectors into short codes with fast distance tables; HNSW (Malkov and Yashunin, 2016) builds a navigable multi-layer proximity graph with logarithmic-like search; FAISS (Johnson, Douze and Jégou, 2017) put both on GPUs; ScaNN (Guo et al., ICML 2020) quantizes with a loss aimed at preserving inner products rather than distances. Facebook's embedding-based retrieval for search (Huang et al., KDD 2020) is a candid production account, including the finding that random negatives beat negatives drawn from impressed-but-not-clicked results for retrieval, because retrieval must separate relevant from irrelevant rather than good from slightly better. Graph models such as Pinterest's PinSage (Ying et al., KDD 2018), a graph convolutional network over a pin–board graph with billions of nodes, and LightGCN (He et al., SIGIR 2020) supplied retrieval embeddings from the interaction graph.

Multi-task ranking: one model, several outcomes

Ranking models moved from predicting one outcome to predicting several (click, like, share, watch time, conversion) with shared lower layers, and two papers in 2018 defined the patterns.

ESMM fixes conversion-rate selection bias by never training CVR on clicks alone. Conversion is observed only after a click, so a CVR model trained on clicked impressions learns P(\text{conv}\mid\text{click},x) from a sample that differs from the impressions it must score, and has few positives. Ma et al. (SIGIR 2018, Alibaba) model over all impressions:

\underbrace{P(\text{click}\wedge\text{conv}\mid x)}_{\text{pCTCVR}}=\underbrace{P(\text{click}\mid x)}_{\text{pCTR}}\times\underbrace{P(\text{conv}\mid\text{click},x)}_{\text{pCVR}},

with losses on pCTR (label: click) and pCTCVR (label: click and conversion), both defined on every impression. The pCVR tower receives gradients only through the product, and shares embeddings with the data-rich CTR task.

MMoE lets tasks share experts with learned, per-task mixing. Ma et al. (KDD 2018, Google) use n expert networks f_i and a softmax gate per task k:

y_k=h^k\Bigl(\sum_{i=1}^{n}g^k(x)_i\,f_i(x)\Bigr),\qquad g^k(x)=\operatorname{softmax}(W_{g_k}x).

Weakly related tasks can use different experts, which reduces the negative transfer of a single shared bottom. YouTube's 2019 ranking system (Zhao et al., RecSys 2019) combined MMoE with a shallow “position tower” to absorb position bias (chapter 7); Tencent's PLE (Tang et al., RecSys 2020) separated shared and task-specific experts. Final ranking typically combines task predictions with a hand-tuned or learned formula, which is where product policy enters the model.

The reproducibility crisis: many neural gains were weak baselines

Several audits from 2019 to 2022 found that a large share of published neural recommendation gains disappeared against properly tuned classical baselines.

  • Dacrema, Cremonesi and Jannach (RecSys 2019) tried to reproduce 18 neural methods from top venues; only 7 could be reproduced with reasonable effort, and 6 of those were often beaten by simple, well-tuned neighbourhood or linear methods.
  • Rendle, Krichene, Zhang and Anderson (RecSys 2020) revisited neural collaborative filtering (He et al., WWW 2017): a carefully tuned dot-product model beat the learned MLP similarity, and an MLP needed large capacity to approximate a dot product.
  • Krichene and Rendle (KDD 2020) showed that evaluating against a small sample of random negatives, a common shortcut, can reverse the ranking of methods relative to full-corpus metrics.

How to read industrial results after this

Industrial papers report online A/B gains on internal baselines, which cannot be audited. They remain the best available evidence of what worked at that company, but treat “+X% engagement” as conditional on an unknown baseline, unknown metric definition and unknown duration. The same caution applies, more strongly, to Era V claims in chapter 8.

What broke next

The deep stack worked, but three problems were left: models trained on their own biased logs; a pricing layer still built for manual bidders; and, from 2017, regulators and platform owners cutting off the cross-site identity signal these models consumed.
