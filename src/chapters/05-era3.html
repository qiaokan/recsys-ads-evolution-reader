Era III (2013–2020): embeddings, deep ranking and the multi-stage funnel

In this chapter

  • Draw the retrieval → ranking → re-ranking funnel and say why it exists.
  • Draw a two-tower model and say which algorithms need approximate nearest-neighbour search (most do not).
  • Derive why YouTube's watch-time-weighted logistic regression predicts expected watch time.
  • Compare the explicit-interaction architectures (Wide & Deep, DeepFM, the Deep & Cross Network or DCN, Meta's Deep Learning Recommendation Model or DLRM) by what they cross and at what cost.
  • Explain the candidate-aware attention of the Deep Interest Network (DIN), two-tower retrieval with its popularity (logQ) correction, and how the Entire Space Multi-task Model (ESMM) reduces selection bias in conversion-rate prediction.
  • Summarize the reproducibility audits that deflated many neural gains.
  • Terms are defined at first use here and collected in the glossary.

The gist. Deep learning entered recommendation through embeddings: every id, word and category became a learned dense vector, and neural networks learned the interactions engineers used to hand-code. Because no network can score billions of items per request, systems split into a funnel: a cheap retrieval stage (two towers whose dot product can be searched with approximate nearest neighbours) narrows millions of items to thousands, and an expensive ranking network orders those. Ranking models grew explicit cross layers, attention over each user's behaviour history and multiple task heads. A wave of reproducibility studies then showed that many published neural gains came from weak baselines, a corrective worth carrying into Era V.

The funnel: spend compute where the decision is close

Every large recommender since about 2016 is a cascade of increasingly expensive models over decreasing candidate sets, because exact scoring of the whole corpus with the best model is unaffordable within the latency budget.

The design consequence is that each stage optimizes a different proxy. Retrieval is judged by recall of what the ranker would have chosen; ranking by accurate and calibrated scores (predicted probabilities that match observed rates, since ads multiply them by bids) on a biased candidate set; re-ranking by list-level objectives. Mismatch between stages (retrieval discarding items the ranker would love) is a persistent source of loss that Era V's generative models try to remove.

Embeddings: ids become vectors

The first deep idea to stick was representing each discrete entity as a learned vector, trained so that entities appearing in similar contexts get similar vectors. Microsoft's Deep Structured Semantic Model, DSSM (Huang et al., CIKM 2013), mapped queries and documents through separate networks into a shared space and trained on clicks with a softmax over one clicked document and a few random ones; this is the two-tower architecture in its first form. After word2vec (Mikolov et al., 2013), the skip-gram objective was applied to sequences of items instead of words: purchase sequences in e-mail receipts (prod2vec, Grbovic et al., KDD 2015), item co-occurrence (item2vec, 2016), and booking sessions at Airbnb (Grbovic and Cheng, KDD 2018). Embeddings made similarity search over items cheap and transferable across tasks.

YouTube 2016: the template for deep recommendation

Covington, Adams and Sargin (RecSys 2016) set the template: a deep candidate generator trained as extreme multiclass classification, and a deep ranker trained to predict watch time.

  • Candidate generation treats “which video will this user watch next” as a softmax over millions of classes, P(w_t=i\mid U,C)=e^{\mathbf v_i^{\top}\mathbf u}/\sum_j e^{\mathbf v_j^{\top}\mathbf u}, where w_t is the video watched at time t, U the user, C the context, \mathbf v_i a learned output embedding for video i, and \mathbf u the output of a network over averaged watch and search embeddings, demographics and context. Training uses sampled softmax, which normalizes over a sample of negative videos instead of all millions (the correction this needs is in two-tower retrieval below); serving becomes a nearest-neighbour search for the largest \mathbf v_i^{\top}\mathbf u.
  • Freshness: an “example age” feature lets the model represent how popularity depends on time since upload; at serving it is set to zero (or slightly negative), so the model does not systematically favour old content.
  • Label design: predict the next watch rather than a randomly held-out one, because viewing is sequential (episode 2 follows episode 1).
  • Ranking uses logistic regression in which each clicked impression is weighted by its watch time T_i and each unclicked impression has weight 1.
Derivation

Why the weighted odds estimate expected watch time

Given: N impressions in some feature cell (impressions sharing the same feature values \mathbf x), k of them clicked with watch times T_1,\dots,T_k (T=0 for unclicked impressions); click probability \bar p=k/N; expected watch time per impression \mathbb E[T]=\sum_iT_i/N.

  1. A logistic regression fitted to weighted data learns odds equal to (weighted positives)/(weighted negatives) in the cell: \text{odds}=e^{\mathbf w^{\top}\mathbf x+b}=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^kT_i}{N-k}.
  2. Divide numerator and denominator by N: \text{odds}=\frac{\mathbb E[T]}{1-\bar p}\approx\mathbb E[T]\,(1+\bar p)\approx\mathbb E[T]\quad\text{for small }\bar p.

Result: serving e^{\mathbf w^{\top}\mathbf x+b} instead of the sigmoid ranks by approximately expected watch time.

Check: with N=100, k=5, total watch 300 s: \mathbb E[T]=3 s, odds =300/95=3.16 s =3/(1-0.05).

Optimizing watch time instead of clicks was a deliberate objective change (YouTube had announced in March 2012 that related and recommended videos would weight time watched more heavily) aimed at click-bait thumbnails. It also illustrates the next problem: any single engagement proxy can be gamed, which led to multi-task objectives below and to the satisfaction-based objectives in chapter 7.

Learned feature interactions: from Wide & Deep to DLRM

The ranking-model literature of 2016–2021 is a search for architectures that learn feature crosses an engineer would otherwise hand-code, at acceptable serving cost. A feature cross is a feature built from a combination of others, such as user country × ad category, so the model can learn that the combination matters more than its parts. Inputs are dozens to hundreds of categorical fields (user id, ad id, country, device), each field f mapped to an embedding \mathbf e_f\in\mathbb R^d, plus dense numeric features. In the cross-layer formulas below, \mathbf x_0\in\mathbb R^D is the concatenated input vector (all field embeddings and dense features), \mathbf x_l the output of layer l, and \mathbf w_l,\mathbf b_l (vectors) or W_l (a matrix) are learned weights.

Why explicit crosses at all? An MLP can approximate multiplication but learns it inefficiently from sparse data; Rendle et al. (2020) showed that an MLP struggles even to learn a dot product. Each DCN layer raises the polynomial degree of the represented crosses by one, so L layers represent crosses of degree up to L+1, at a parameter cost per layer linear in D, the length of \mathbf x_0, for DCN and quadratic in D (or low-rank) for DCN-V2.

The systems fact that shaped all of these: embeddings dominate. A model with hundreds of millions of ids per field has embedding tables of hundreds of gigabytes to terabytes while the dense layers are small. Training therefore moved from CPU parameter servers (Li et al., OSDI 2014) to GPU systems that shard embedding tables across devices and exchange looked-up rows (all-to-all) while replicating the MLPs; DLRM's paper describes exactly this hybrid parallelism, later packaged in libraries such as NVIDIA HugeCTR and Meta's TorchRec.

Behaviour sequences: attention keyed on the candidate

Alibaba's Deep Interest Network, DIN (Zhou et al., KDD 2018), replaced the average of a user's past-behaviour embeddings with a weighted sum whose weights depend on the candidate ad, because a user's history contains many unrelated interests. For candidate ad embedding \mathbf v_A and history embeddings \mathbf e_1,\dots,\mathbf e_H:

\mathbf v_U(A)=\sum_{j=1}^{H}a(\mathbf e_j,\mathbf v_A)\,\mathbf e_j,

where a(\cdot) is a small feed-forward network. The weights are deliberately not softmax-normalized, so a user with many relevant behaviours produces a larger vector than one with a single weak match. Someone who bought a swimsuit and a laptop bag is represented differently when the candidate is goggles than when it is a mouse. The line continued with DIEN, the Deep Interest Evolution Network (AAAI 2019: a gated recurrent unit, GRU, models how interests evolve), and SIM, the Search-based Interest Model (CIKM 2020: first retrieve the relevant subset of a very long, lifelong behaviour sequence, then attend over that subset), which pushed usable history from hundreds of events to tens of thousands (up to about 54,000).

In parallel, session-based recommenders, which predict from the sequence of actions in the current visit (a session), borrowed language-model architectures. GRU4Rec (Hidasi et al., ICLR 2016) predicted the next click in a session with a recurrent network; SASRec, self-attentive sequential recommendation (Kang and McAuley, ICDM 2018), used causal self-attention; BERT4Rec (Sun et al., CIKM 2019) used a bidirectional masked objective. Replication then complicated the ranking. Petrov and Macdonald (RecSys 2022) found published BERT4Rec-versus-SASRec comparisons inconsistent: BERT4Rec needs up to 30 times longer training, many later papers used an under-trained version, and properly trained it does beat SASRec. Klenitskiy and Vasilev (RecSys 2023) then showed that SASRec trained with a full-softmax cross-entropy loss, instead of its original sampled binary loss, outperforms BERT4Rec. Loss and training budget mattered as much as architecture. Sequence modelling returned at a far larger scale in Era V.

Two-tower retrieval and the logQ correction

A two-tower model is two separate neural networks, one for the user (with context) and one for the item, each producing a vector of the same length; the score is their dot product, and nothing else connects them. The user tower reads only user and context features (watch history, recent searches, country, device, time of day); the item tower reads only item features (id, text, category, creator). Because the item tower never sees the user, every item's vector can be computed once, offline, and stored. At request time the system runs the user tower once and looks for the items whose vectors have the largest dot product with it. That lookup is a nearest-neighbour search, which is why two-tower models are deployed together with approximate nearest-neighbour (ANN) indexes.

Two-tower (a bi-encoder: no interaction before the score)

User and item features meet only in the final dot product. Cheap: item vectors are precomputed and indexed, so millions of items can be searched per request. Limited in two ways: features defined on the (user, item) pair, such as “how many of this creator's videos this user has watched”, cannot be inputs to either tower; and all interaction must pass through one d-dimensional inner product. (Context such as the day of week can still change the user vector.) Used for retrieval.

Ranker (full interaction)

User, item, pair and context features are concatenated and crossed from the first layer (DCN, DIN and the other models above). Expressive, but every (user, item) pair needs a full forward pass, so it can score only hundreds to thousands of candidates per request and cannot use an index. Used for ranking.

Training a two-tower model is a classification over the whole catalogue, and that is where a correction for popularity is needed.

Write s(x,y)=\mathbf u(x)^{\top}\mathbf v(y), where x is the user and context (the query), y an item, and \mathbf u,\mathbf v the two towers. The ideal loss is a softmax over the whole corpus, which is unaffordable. Using the other items in a training batch as negatives is cheap, but items appear in batches in proportion to their frequency p_j, so popular items are over-represented as negatives and get pushed down too far. Yi et al. (RecSys 2019, YouTube) subtract the log sampling probability from each logit during training. For a training example whose positive (clicked or watched) item is y_i, with the batch's items y_j as candidates:

s^c(x,y_j)=s(x,y_j)-\log p_j,\qquad \mathcal L=-\log\frac{e^{s^c(x,y_i)}}{\sum_{j\in\text{batch}}e^{s^c(x,y_j)}}.

The name comes from the sampling (proposal) distribution, conventionally written Q; here Q(j)=p_j. This is the standard importance-sampling correction for sampled softmax: dividing each sampled term by its proposal probability makes the batch sum an estimate of the full-corpus normalizer. The paper estimates p_j on the fly from a stream of item ids by tracking the average gap between successive occurrences of each item. At serving time the raw score s(x,y) is used.

Approximate nearest-neighbour search made retrieval over billions of vectors practical. Product quantization (Jégou, Douze and Schmid, 2011) compresses vectors into short codes with fast distance tables; HNSW, hierarchical navigable small world (Malkov and Yashunin, 2016), builds a navigable multi-layer proximity graph with logarithmic-like search; FAISS, Facebook AI Similarity Search (Johnson, Douze and Jégou, 2017), put both on GPUs; ScaNN, Google's Scalable Nearest Neighbors (Guo et al., ICML 2020), quantizes with a loss aimed at preserving inner products rather than distances. Facebook's embedding-based retrieval (EBR) for search (Huang et al., KDD 2020) is a candid production account, including the finding that random negatives beat negatives drawn from impressed-but-not-clicked results for retrieval, because retrieval must separate relevant from irrelevant rather than good from slightly better. Graph models such as Pinterest's PinSage (Ying et al., KDD 2018), a graph convolutional network over a pin–board graph with billions of nodes, and LightGCN, a simplified graph convolution network (He et al., SIGIR 2020), supplied retrieval embeddings from the interaction graph.

Which algorithms need approximate nearest-neighbour search?

ANN is needed only when a model must find the top-scoring items among a very large corpus at request time and its score is a similarity between precomputed vectors; most of the algorithms in this reader do not need it. The test has two parts: does the score factor into a user vector and an item vector (so items can be indexed in advance), and is the corpus too large to score exhaustively within the latency budget?

Worked example

Why exhaustive scoring is expensive at retrieval scale

Given: N=10^8 items, vectors of length d=64, and 10,000 requests per second (illustrative, not one company's numbers).

  1. Exact scoring of one request: N\cdot d=10^8\times64=6.4\times10^9 multiply-adds, plus selecting the k best of 10^8 scores (k is the number of candidates passed on, say 1,000). Unbatched, each request also reads all N\cdot d stored values, about 12.8 gigabytes at 2 bytes each.
  2. At 10,000 requests per second: 6.4\times10^{13} multiply-adds, about 1.3\times10^{14} FLOPs, per second for retrieval alone, before any ranking. Batched on accelerators this is feasible (a fraction of one GPU's peak in principle, more in practice), and some systems do serve brute-force search this way; on CPUs, at N\ge10^9, or under tight latency, an index is the norm.
  3. A graph or quantization index instead compares the query with a small fraction of the corpus (typically thousands to tens of thousands of vectors, often compressed), several orders of magnitude fewer operations, at the price of occasionally missing a true top item; an index's recall is the fraction of the true top-k items it returns.

Result: at 10^8 items, exact retrieval costs billions of operations and gigabytes of memory traffic per request; an index cuts that by orders of magnitude for a little recall, and batched accelerator search is the main alternative.

Check: with N=10^4 items the exact cost is 6.4\times10^5 multiply-adds per request, trivial on one processor, so a small catalogue needs no index.

Multi-task ranking: one model, several outcomes

Ranking models moved from predicting one outcome to predicting several (click, like, share, watch time, conversion) with shared lower layers, and two papers in 2018 defined the patterns.

ESMM (Entire Space Multi-task Model) reduces conversion-rate selection bias by never training the conversion-rate model on clicks alone. A conversion is the action the advertiser wants after the click (a purchase, an install); the conversion rate (CVR) is conversions per click, and the click-through rate (CTR) is clicks per impression. Conversion is observed only after a click, so a CVR model trained on clicked impressions learns P(\text{conv}\mid\text{click},x) from a sample that differs from the impressions it must score, and has few positives. Ma et al. (SIGIR 2018, Alibaba) model over all impressions:

\underbrace{P(\text{click}\wedge\text{conv}\mid x)}_{\text{pCTCVR}}=\underbrace{P(\text{click}\mid x)}_{\text{pCTR}}\times\underbrace{P(\text{conv}\mid\text{click},x)}_{\text{pCVR}},

with losses on pCTR (label: click) and pCTCVR (label: click and conversion), both defined on every impression. The pCVR tower receives gradients only through the product, and shares embeddings with the data-rich CTR task. This reduces the bias rather than removing it: later work (ESCM², the Entire Space Counterfactual Multi-task Model, Wang et al., SIGIR 2022) showed that ESMM's implied pCVR is itself biased and added counterfactual corrections.

MMoE (Multi-gate Mixture-of-Experts) lets tasks share expert subnetworks with learned, per-task mixing. Ma et al. (KDD 2018, Google) use n expert networks f_1,\dots,f_n shared by all tasks; each task t has a gate g^t, a softmax over experts with its own weight matrix W_{g_t}, and a small task-specific network (a tower) h^t that produces the prediction y_t:

y_t=h^t\Bigl(\sum_{i=1}^{n}g^t(x)_i\,f_i(x)\Bigr),\qquad g^t(x)=\operatorname{softmax}(W_{g_t}x).

Weakly related tasks can use different experts, which reduces negative transfer (one task's gradients hurting another task) compared with a shared bottom, a single shared network feeding all task towers. YouTube's 2019 ranking system (Zhao et al., RecSys 2019) combined MMoE with a shallow position-bias tower, a small network that takes the display position as input, to absorb position bias (chapter 7); Tencent's PLE, Progressive Layered Extraction (Tang et al., RecSys 2020), separated shared and task-specific experts. Final ranking typically combines task predictions with a hand-tuned or learned formula, which is where product policy enters the model.

The reproducibility crisis: many neural gains were weak baselines

Several audits from 2019 to 2022 found that a large share of published neural recommendation gains disappeared against properly tuned classical baselines.

  • Dacrema, Cremonesi and Jannach (RecSys 2019) tried to reproduce 18 neural methods from top venues; only 7 could be reproduced with reasonable effort, and 6 of those were often beaten by simple, well-tuned neighbourhood or linear methods.
  • Rendle, Krichene, Zhang and Anderson (RecSys 2020) revisited neural collaborative filtering (He et al., WWW 2017): a carefully tuned dot-product model beat the learned MLP similarity, and an MLP needed large capacity to approximate a dot product.
  • Krichene and Rendle (KDD 2020) showed that evaluating against a small sample of random negatives, a common shortcut, can reverse the ranking of methods relative to full-corpus metrics.

How to read industrial results after this

Industrial papers report online A/B gains on internal baselines, which cannot be audited. They remain the best available evidence of what worked at that company, but treat “+X% engagement” as conditional on an unknown baseline, unknown metric definition and unknown duration. The same caution applies, more strongly, to Era V claims in chapter 8.

What broke next

The deep stack worked, but three problems were left: models trained on their own biased logs; a pricing layer still built for manual bidders; and, from 2017, regulators and platform owners cutting off the cross-site identity signal these models consumed.
