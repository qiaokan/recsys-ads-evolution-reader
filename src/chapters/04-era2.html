Era II (2006–2013): latent factors and planet-scale linear models

In this chapter

  • Say what the Netflix Prize did and did not change, and write the biased matrix-factorization objective with its SGD update.
  • Derive the implicit-feedback ALS trick that makes summing over all unobserved cells affordable.
  • State BPR's pairwise objective and its relation to AUC.
  • Derive the factorization-machine identity that cuts pairwise interactions from O(kn^2) to O(kn).
  • Explain why ad click prediction ran on online logistic regression (AdPredictor, FTRL-Proximal, hashing, GBDT features) and why contextual bandits appeared.

The gist. Two separate communities attacked sparsity. Recommender researchers, galvanized by Netflix's $1M prize, learned a short latent vector per user and per item so that two users who never rated the same film could still be compared. Ad engineers, facing billions of sparse features and models retrained every few minutes, chose the simplest model that could keep up: logistic regression, trained online with per-coordinate learning rates and L1 sparsity, often fed by hashed or tree-derived features. Factorization machines joined the two lines by giving every feature a latent vector. Bandits addressed the remaining gap: a model trained on its own past choices never learns about the options it never shows.

The Netflix Prize turned recommendation into a leaderboard

From October 2006 to September 2009 Netflix offered $1 million to whoever cut the RMSE of its Cinematch rating predictor by 10% on a hidden test set; the competition popularized latent-factor models and ensembling, and showed the limits of RMSE as a business objective. The data were about 100 million ratings (1–5 stars) from about 480,000 users on 17,770 films. Cinematch scored a test RMSE of about 0.9525; the target was 0.8572. Annual progress prizes went to the BellKor team (8.43% in 2007) and to the merged team BellKor in BigChaos (9.44% in 2008), both measured against Cinematch on the public quiz set (RMSE 0.9514). The grand prize went to BellKor's Pragmatic Chaos with a test RMSE of 0.8567, a 10.06% improvement, tying the runner-up's score but submitted earlier (sources).

Netflix adopted two of the component models, not the winning ensemble. Its 2012 engineering post explains that matrix factorization and restricted Boltzmann machines went into production after the first progress prize, but the extra accuracy of the full blend did not justify the engineering cost; by then the product had shifted from mailed DVDs and star ratings to streaming, where what people play matters more than how they rate. The lesson that the competition's offline metric was not the business metric recurs in every later era. A second contest was cancelled in 2010 after researchers showed that the “anonymized” ratings could be linked to public IMDb reviews (Narayanan and Shmatikov, 2008), an early warning for chapter 7.

Matrix factorization: a short vector per user and per item

Matrix factorization models each rating as a global mean plus user and item biases plus a dot product of learned k-dimensional factors, fitted only on observed ratings. Simon Funk's December 2006 blog post showed that plain stochastic gradient descent on the observed entries, with no imputation, moved quickly up the leaderboard; Koren, Bell and Volinsky (IEEE Computer, 2009) is the standard account. With \mathcal K the set of observed (user, item) pairs:

\min_{b,p,q}\sum_{(u,i)\in\mathcal K}\bigl(r_{ui}-\mu-b_u-b_i-\mathbf p_u^{\top}\mathbf q_i\bigr)^2+\lambda\bigl(b_u^2+b_i^2+\lVert\mathbf p_u\rVert^2+\lVert\mathbf q_i\rVert^2\bigr).

For one observed rating with error e_{ui}=r_{ui}-\hat r_{ui} and step size \gamma, the SGD update is

\begin{aligned}b_u&\leftarrow b_u+\gamma(e_{ui}-\lambda b_u), & \mathbf p_u&\leftarrow\mathbf p_u+\gamma(e_{ui}\,\mathbf q_i-\lambda\mathbf p_u),\\ b_i&\leftarrow b_i+\gamma(e_{ui}-\lambda b_i), & \mathbf q_i&\leftarrow\mathbf q_i+\gamma(e_{ui}\,\mathbf p_u-\lambda\mathbf q_i).\end{aligned}

(The constant factor 2 from differentiating the square is absorbed into \gamma.) Why it generalizes where neighbourhoods do not: two users who share no films still get comparable vectors if their films' vectors are similar, because the item vectors are shared across everyone. The Prize then drove a series of refinements by Koren: SVD++ (KDD 2008) adds a term built from which films a user rated, regardless of the rating, an implicit signal; timeSVD++ (KDD 2009) lets biases and factors drift over time.

Pitfall: “SVD” is a misnomer here

Netflix-era “SVD” is not the singular value decomposition: SVD is undefined on a matrix with 99% missing entries, and imputing zeros would treat “not rated” as “hated”. The model is a regularized low-rank fit on observed cells only.

Implicit feedback: every unobserved cell matters, and ALS makes that affordable

For clicks, plays and purchases there are no negative ratings, so the model must use the unobserved cells; Hu, Koren and Volinsky (ICDM 2008) made this tractable with a weighted least-squares trick. Define a binary preference p_{ui}=1 if user u consumed item i at all, else 0, and a confidence c_{ui}=1+\alpha r_{ui} that grows with the amount consumed r_{ui}. The loss sums over all M\times N cells:

\min_{X,Y}\sum_{u,i}c_{ui}\,\bigl(p_{ui}-\mathbf x_u^{\top}\mathbf y_i\bigr)^2+\lambda\Bigl(\sum_u\lVert\mathbf x_u\rVert^2+\sum_i\lVert\mathbf y_i\rVert^2\Bigr).

With item factors Y (an N\times k matrix) fixed, each user's vector has a closed form, \mathbf x_u=(Y^{\top}C^uY+\lambda I)^{-1}Y^{\top}C^u\mathbf p_u, where C^u is diagonal with entries c_{ui}. Computed naively, Y^{\top}C^uY costs O(k^2N) per user. The trick:

Y^{\top}C^uY=\underbrace{Y^{\top}Y}_{\text{same for all users}}+\;Y^{\top}(C^u-I)\,Y,

and C^u-I is nonzero only on the n_u items the user consumed. So one O(k^2N) computation of Y^{\top}Y per sweep is shared, and each user costs O(k^2n_u+k^3). A full sweep over users costs O(k^2|\mathcal K|+k^3M), linear in the number of observed interactions, even though the loss touches every cell. Alternating with the symmetric item step gives alternating least squares (ALS), which parallelizes trivially and became the standard implicit-feedback baseline (Spark MLlib's ALS implements it).

BPR: optimize the ranking, not the reconstruction

Bayesian Personalized Ranking (Rendle et al., UAI 2009) trains on pairs: a consumed item should score above an unconsumed one for the same user. For triples (u,i,j) with i observed and j sampled from the unobserved items, and any scoring model \hat x_{ui}:

\max_\Theta\sum_{(u,i,j)}\ln\sigma\bigl(\hat x_{ui}-\hat x_{uj}\bigr)-\lambda\lVert\Theta\rVert^2,\qquad \sigma(z)=\frac1{1+e^{-z}}.

The per-user AUC is the fraction of (observed, unobserved) pairs ranked correctly, \frac1{|P_u|}\sum\mathbb 1[\hat x_{ui}>\hat x_{uj}]; BPR replaces the step function with the smooth surrogate \ln\sigma. Sampling negatives instead of summing over all of them is the idea that returns, at much larger scale, as in-batch negatives in two-tower retrieval.

Click prediction for ads: logistic regression that never stops training

Ad click models of this era were logistic regressions over billions of sparse binary features, trained online, because freshness and calibration mattered more than model class. A feature is typically an indicator for a value or a cross of values (query word × ad id, user country × advertiser). The model is p(\text{click}\mid\mathbf x)=\sigma(\mathbf w^{\top}\mathbf x), trained on log loss. Landmarks:

  • Microsoft, 2007. Richardson, Dominowska and Ragno (WWW 2007) predicted click rates for new search ads from ad text and term features, the cold-start problem of the quality-weighted auction.
  • Bing's AdPredictor, 2010. Graepel et al. (ICML 2010) used Bayesian probit regression with a Gaussian belief per weight, updated online by approximate message passing in a factor graph; it served all of Bing's sponsored-search traffic from 2009. The posterior variance doubles as an uncertainty estimate, useful for exploration.
  • Feature hashing, 2009. Weinberger et al. (ICML 2009) mapped raw feature strings to a fixed-size weight vector with a hash function, so the model never needs a feature dictionary; collisions behave like a small amount of noise. This became the default way to handle open-ended vocabularies.
  • Google's FTRL-Proximal, 2013. McMahan et al., “Ad click prediction: a view from the trenches” (KDD 2013), is the reference for production practice.
  • Facebook, 2014. He et al. (ADKDD 2014) fed the leaf indices of boosted decision trees into a logistic regression: the trees learn non-linear feature crosses offline, the linear model trains online. The paper also showed that prediction quality decayed measurably as the model went stale, so retraining frequency was a first-order design choice.

FTRL-Proximal gives per-coordinate learning rates with exact zeros, so the model stays sparse enough to serve. For each coordinate i, keep a running sum of squared gradients n_i and an adjusted gradient sum z_i; with gradient g_i on the current example:

\begin{aligned}\sigma_i&=\frac{\sqrt{n_i+g_i^2}-\sqrt{n_i}}{\alpha},\qquad z_i\leftarrow z_i+g_i-\sigma_i w_i,\qquad n_i\leftarrow n_i+g_i^2,\\ w_i&=\begin{cases}0 & |z_i|\le\lambda_1,\\[2pt] -\dfrac{z_i-\operatorname{sgn}(z_i)\,\lambda_1}{(\beta+\sqrt{n_i})/\alpha+\lambda_2} & \text{otherwise.}\end{cases}\end{aligned}

The learning rate for coordinate i is effectively \alpha/(\beta+\sqrt{n_i}): rare features (small n_i) take large steps, common ones small steps. The L1 threshold \lambda_1 sets a weight to exactly zero until its accumulated evidence exceeds the threshold, which plain online gradient descent with L1 does not achieve. The paper's other lessons were systems lessons: encode weights in fewer bits, admit new features probabilistically, and monitor calibration per slice, because the auction multiplies the prediction by the bid (chapter 3).

Factorization machines: a latent vector for every feature

Factorization machines (Rendle, ICDM 2010) generalize both matrix factorization and logistic regression by modelling every pairwise feature interaction as a dot product of learned vectors, at linear cost.

\hat y(\mathbf x)=w_0+\sum_{i=1}^n w_ix_i+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=i+1}^{n}\langle\mathbf v_i,\mathbf v_j\rangle\,x_ix_j,\qquad\mathbf v_i\in\mathbb R^k.

A cross such as (user = 42, ad = 7) that never appeared in training still gets a weight, \langle\mathbf v_{42},\mathbf v_7\rangle, learned from each feature's other co-occurrences; logistic regression on explicit crosses would give it zero. With only a user one-hot and an item one-hot as input, the FM reduces to biased matrix factorization.

Derivation

From O(kn^2) to O(kn)

  1. Sum over all ordered pairs and remove the diagonal: \sum_{i<j}\langle\mathbf v_i,\mathbf v_j\rangle x_ix_j=\tfrac12\Bigl(\sum_{i,j}\langle\mathbf v_i,\mathbf v_j\rangle x_ix_j-\sum_i\langle\mathbf v_i,\mathbf v_i\rangle x_i^2\Bigr).
  2. Expand the dot product over the k factor dimensions and factor the double sum: =\tfrac12\sum_{f=1}^{k}\Bigl[\Bigl(\sum_i v_{i,f}x_i\Bigr)^2-\sum_i v_{i,f}^2x_i^2\Bigr].
  3. Each inner sum touches only the nonzero x_i; with m nonzeros the cost is O(km).

Check with k=1, v=(1,2,3), x=(1,1,1): direct sum 1\cdot2+1\cdot3+2\cdot3=11; formula \tfrac12\bigl[(1+2+3)^2-(1+4+9)\bigr]=\tfrac12(36-14)=11.

Field-aware FMs won the CTR leaderboards. Juan et al. (RecSys 2016) gave each feature one vector per field it interacts with (a user feature uses a different vector when crossing with the ad field than with the time field). Variants of FFM won the Criteo and Avazu Kaggle click-prediction challenges in 2014–2015. The cost is F times more parameters for F fields, a trade-off later deep models revisited.

Bandits: the model only learns about what it shows

A model trained on its own logs never sees outcomes for items it never displayed, so this era borrowed contextual bandits to trade off exploiting the best estimate against exploring uncertain ones. Li, Chu, Langford and Schapire (WWW 2010) ran LinUCB on the Yahoo! front-page news module. For each article a with context vector \mathbf x, keep a ridge regression with A_a=D_a^{\top}D_a+I and estimate \hat{\boldsymbol\theta}_a=A_a^{-1}D_a^{\top}\mathbf c_a, where D_a stacks past contexts for that article and \mathbf c_a their click outcomes; then show

a^*=\arg\max_a\Bigl(\mathbf x^{\top}\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}_a+\alpha\sqrt{\mathbf x^{\top}A_a^{-1}\mathbf x}\Bigr).

The second term is a confidence width that shrinks as an article accumulates impressions in that region of context space. Two contributions outlived the algorithm. First, replay evaluation: with logs collected under uniformly random display, a new policy can be evaluated offline by keeping only the events where it agrees with the logged choice (Li et al., WSDM 2011); this is the root of the off-policy methods in chapter 7. Second, Chapelle and Li (NIPS 2011) showed empirically that Thompson sampling, drawing a parameter from the posterior and acting greedily on the draw, matched or beat UCB on ad and news data and was easier to implement with delayed batch updates. It became a common exploration method in ad systems.

What broke next

Linear models made engineers the bottleneck: every useful interaction had to be hand-specified as a feature cross. FMs learned pairwise crosses automatically but not higher-order or non-linear ones; factor models ignored the rich side information (text, images, sequences) that platforms now had. Meanwhile GPUs and the 2012 ImageNet result made deep networks practical. Era III replaced feature engineering with learned embeddings and interaction layers, and replaced the single scoring pass with a funnel.
