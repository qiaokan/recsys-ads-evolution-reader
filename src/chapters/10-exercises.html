Exercises with worked answers

In this chapter

  • Gist set (seven problems, 30 minutes): the conceptual core of each era, with light arithmetic.
  • Depth set (nine problems, 30 minutes): the derivations from chapters 4–7 and 9, with numbers you can check by hand.
  • Try each before opening the answer; every answer ends with a check.
  • Terms are defined at first use here and collected in the glossary.

The gist. If you can price a quality-weighted auction, de-bias a click log, explain why an autobidder may bid above value, grade a claim by its evidence and say where an LLM can afford to run, you have the gist path. The depth set tests whether you can redo the key derivations: the implicit-ALS saving, the watch-time odds, the logQ correction, budget multipliers, bid shading, delayed feedback, ESMM and the cost of an LLM ranker.

Gist set

G1 · Price a quality-weighted auction (chapter 3)

Problem. One slot. Ad A bids $3.00 per click with predicted CTR 0.02; ad B bids $1.50 with CTR 0.05; ad C bids $2.00 with CTR 0.01. Who wins under Google's 2002 rule, what is the price per click, and what is the expected revenue per impression?

Answer. Scores b\cdot c (bid per click times predicted click-through rate, the expected revenue per impression): A =\$0.060, B =\$0.075, C =\$0.020. B wins. Price p_B=b_Ac_A/c_B=0.060/0.05=\$1.20 per click. Expected revenue per impression 0.05\times\$1.20=\$0.060.

Check: expected revenue equals the runner-up's score, as in chapter 3. Under GoTo's pay-your-bid rule ranked by bid, A would win and pay $3.00 per click, for expected revenue 0.02\times3=\$0.060: the same here, but with 40% of B's clicks and an unstable bidding game.

G2 · Why item–item at Amazon (chapter 3)

Problem. Give two reasons Amazon chose item-to-item filtering over user–user filtering.

Answer. (1) Serving cost: with a precomputed similar-items table, the online cost depends only on the number of items the current user has bought, not on the number of customers or items. (2) Stability: item–item similarities change slowly and can be recomputed in batch, whereas a user's neighbourhood changes with every purchase and is noisy for users with few purchases.

Check: with N items and M customers, the offline table costs O(N^2M) in the worst case, close to O(NM) in practice. The expensive work is moved offline, not removed.

G3 · What the Netflix Prize taught (chapter 4)

Problem. Netflix paid $1M for a 10.06% RMSE improvement but did not deploy the winning ensemble. Why, and what is the general lesson?

Answer. The last few points of RMSE came from blending hundreds of models, which was expensive to engineer and maintain, and the business had moved to streaming, where what people play (implicit feedback, ranking of a page) mattered more than predicting star ratings. Netflix adopted two components (matrix factorization and RBMs). Lesson: an offline metric on a fixed dataset is a proxy, and the gap between proxy and business objective grows as the product changes.

Check: the same lesson reappears in chapter 5's reproducibility audits and chapter 7's moving objectives.

G4 · De-bias a click log (chapter 7)

Problem. Examination probabilities, the chance a user looks at each position at all (chapter 7), are \theta_1=1.0 and \theta_2=0.6. Item D was shown 1,000 times at position 1 and clicked 70 times; item C was shown 1,000 times at position 2 and clicked 45 times. Which item is more relevant?

Answer. Naive CTR: D 0.070, C 0.045. IPS-corrected relevance: D =70/1.0/1{,}000=0.070; C =45/0.6/1{,}000=0.075. C is more relevant and should be tried at position 1.

Check: if C were at position 1 its expected clicks would be 1{,}000\times1.0\times0.075=75>70.

G5 · An autobidder bids above value (chapter 6)

Problem. Target CPA $50, pCTR 0.02, pCVR 0.05 (per click). Compute the value per 1,000 impressions. The campaign's budget is not binding and its learned bid multiplier is \kappa=1.2 (the \kappa=(1+\lambda)/(\lambda+\mu) of chapter 6, with \lambda the target constraint's multiplier and \mu the budget's). What does it bid in a second-price auction, and why can that be consistent with the advertiser's target?

Answer. Value per impression 0.02\times0.05\times\$50=\$0.05, i.e. $50 per 1,000. Bid 1.2\times\$50=\$60 CPM. The target constrains the average cost per conversion. In a second-price auction many impressions clear well below the bid, and the slack they create pays for impressions won above value, so the campaign buys more conversions while still averaging $50 per conversion.

Check: with \mu=0, \kappa=(1+\lambda)/\lambda=1.2 gives \lambda=5; any \lambda>0 gives \kappa>1.

G6 · Grade the evidence (chapter 1)

Problem. Grade each: (a) “HSTU is a sequential transducer with pointwise rather than softmax attention.” (b) “A new ads model drove a 5% increase in conversions on Instagram last quarter,” said on an earnings call. (c) “Platforms with closed ecosystems lost less signal from ATT.”

Answer. (a) Paper: an architectural fact stated in the ICML 2024 paper. (b) Call: management's attribution of a business metric; baseline, duration and measurement method undisclosed. (c) Inference: plausible from the mechanism, not a measured result in a cited source.

Check: only (a) could be verified by someone outside the company, by reading the paper and code.

G7 · Where does the LLM run? (chapter 9)

Problem. For each use, say whether the LLM runs per item (offline), per query (cached or tail-only), or per candidate per request, and whether that is affordable at 10,000 requests per second: (a) embedding every new video's title and description; (b) rewriting rare search queries; (c) scoring 500 ranked candidates for every request with a 7-billion-parameter model; (d) reviewing every newly submitted ad against policy.

Answer. (a) Per item, offline: affordable, the cost is paid once per item and cached. (b) Per query, cached for frequent queries with live inference only for the rare tail (Instacart sends about 2% of traffic to the live model): affordable. (c) Per candidate per request: about 7\times10^{18} FLOPs per second in chapter 9's example, thousands of accelerators for ranking alone: not affordable; distil instead. (d) Per ad, at submission, with minutes of latency allowed: affordable.

Check: the affordable uses are exactly those whose cost does not multiply by candidates × requests.

Depth set

D1 · How much the implicit-ALS trick saves (chapter 4)

Problem. N=10^6 items, k=64 factors, and a user with |\mathcal K_u|=50 interactions. In the notation of chapter 4, compare the cost of forming Q^{\top}C^uQ naively with the trick (counting multiply-adds, plus k^3 for the solve in both cases).

Answer. Naive: k^2N+k^3=4{,}096\times10^6+262{,}144\approx4.1\times10^9. Trick: k^2|\mathcal K_u|+k^3=4{,}096\times50+262{,}144=204{,}800+262{,}144=466{,}944, with Q^{\top}Q computed once per sweep for all users. Ratio about 8{,}800\times.

Check: the shared Q^{\top}Q costs the same 4.1\times10^9 once, amortized over millions of users.

D2 · Watch-time odds (chapter 5)

Problem. In a feature cell, 200 impressions, 10 clicks, total watch time 1,500 s. What odds does the weighted logistic regression learn, and how close is it to expected watch time per impression?

Answer. Odds =1{,}500/(200-10)=7.89 s. \mathbb E[T]=1{,}500/200=7.5 s and P=0.05, so odds =7.5/0.95=7.89, about 5% above \mathbb E[T].

Check: the approximation \mathbb E[T](1+P)=7.875 is about 0.25% below the exact 7.89.

D3 · Why logQ correction works (chapter 5)

Problem. Items A and B both have raw score s=1. A appears in batches with frequency p_A=0.02, B with p_B=0.0002. Compute each item's corrected contribution e^{s-\log p} to the softmax denominator when it appears, and the expected contribution per batch slot.

Answer. A: e^{1-\ln0.02}=e^{1+3.912}=e^{4.912}=135.9. B: e^{1-\ln0.0002}=e^{1+8.517}=e^{9.517}=13{,}590. Expected contribution per slot: p_A\times135.9=2.718 and p_B\times13{,}590=2.718, both e^{1}.

Check: in general \sum_jp_j\,e^{s_j-\log p_j}=\sum_je^{s_j}, the full-corpus normalizer. Without the correction, A would contribute 100 times more often than B as a negative, and the model would learn to push popular items down.

D4 · Find the budget multiplier (chapter 6)

Problem. Four impressions with (value, second-price cost) in dollars: (10, 4), (8, 6), (6, 2), (5, 4). Budget $8. Which impressions does the bid v/(1+\mu) buy, for what range of \mu, and is that optimal?

Answer. Win iff v/p\ge1+\mu. Ratios: 2.5, 1.33, 3.0, 1.25. Without a budget (\mu=0) all four are bought for $16 > $8. For 1.33<1+\mu\le2.5 the policy buys impressions 1 and 3, spends $6, surplus (10-4)+(6-2)=\$10. Enumerating feasible sets with spend ≤ $8: {1,3} $10; {1,4} $7; {2,3} $6; {3,4} $5; so it is optimal.

Check: $2 of budget is unspent because impressions are indivisible; with many small impressions the threshold policy spends almost exactly B.

D5 · Shade a first-price bid (chapter 6)

Problem. Value v=\$5 CPM. (a) Highest competing bid Z\sim\text{Uniform}(0,\$8): optimal bid, win probability and expected surplus per 1,000 auctions, and compare with second price. (b) If Z is the maximum of two independent Uniform(0, $8) bids, F(b)=(b/8)^2: optimal bid?

Answer. (a) b^*=v/2=\$2.50; F=2.5/8=0.3125; surplus (5-2.5)\times0.3125=\$0.78. Second price, bid $5: v^2/(2L) with L=\$8 gives 25/16=\$1.56. (b) F/f=(b^2/64)/(2b/64)=b/2, so 5-b=b/2, b^*=2v/3=\$3.33.

Check: more competition shades less (2/3 of value instead of 1/2). With n uniform competitors, b^*=nv/(n+1), approaching truthful bidding.

D6 · A click that has not converted yet (chapter 7)

Problem. Eventual conversion probability p=0.04; delay exponential with rate h=0.5 per day (mean 2 days). A click has waited \tau=1 day without converting. What is the likelihood of that observation, and the probability it will still convert?

Answer. e^{-0.5}=0.6065. Likelihood 1-p+pe^{-h\tau}=0.96+0.04\times0.6065=0.9843. Probability of eventual conversion given none yet: pe^{-h\tau}/0.9843=0.02426/0.9843=0.0246.

Check: the click still carries 62% of its prior conversion probability (0.0246/0.04). Labelling it negative would throw that away.

D7 · ESMM by hand (chapter 5)

Problem. For an impression, pCTR = 0.02 and pCTCVR = 0.001. What is pCVR? Why is a CVR model trained only on clicked impressions biased when used to score all impressions?

Answer. \text{pCVR}=0.001/0.02=0.05. Clicked impressions are a selected sample: users who click differ systematically from those who do not, so a model fitted on them learns P(\text{conv}\mid\text{click},x) only where clicks are common and extrapolates elsewhere. ESMM trains pCTR and pCTCVR on all impressions, and pCVR only through their ratio, which reduces (though, as chapter 5 notes, does not fully remove) the bias.

Check: 0.02\times0.05=0.001; with 1,000,000 impressions that is 20,000 clicks and 1,000 conversions.

D8 · Pre-mortem a generative-ads claim (chapters 7–8)

Problem. A platform reports that a new foundation ranking model raised ad conversions by 5%. Twelve months later, advertisers' own holdout tests (randomized groups not shown the ads, chapter 7) show no change in incremental sales. Give three mechanisms that would reconcile both facts.

Answer. (1) Attribution, not incrementality: the model got better at reaching people who would have converted anyway, raising attributed conversions without causing more. (2) Measurement by the optimizer: reported conversions include modelled conversions from the same platform, so the metric partly moves with the model. (3) Mix and novelty: the A/B test ran on a favourable slice or a short window; gains decayed as advertisers' bids, budgets and competitors adapted (the auction is an equilibrium, not a fixed environment).

Check: each mechanism predicts something testable: (1) attributed and incremental conversions diverge in a ghost-ad holdout (one that logs which ads the control group would have seen); (2) observed-only conversions rise less than reported ones; (3) the lift shrinks with test duration.

D9 · The price of an LLM ranker, and of distilling it (chapter 9)

Problem. A 3-billion-parameter LLM would score each candidate from 50 tokens. (a) FLOPs per candidate, using \text{FLOPs}\approx2PT? (b) With 300 candidates per request and 5,000 requests per second, total FLOPs per second? (c) Instead, the LLM labels 100 million query–item pairs once (same 50 tokens each) and a student model costing 10^7 FLOPs per candidate serves. How many seconds of serving at that traffic does the one-time labeling cost equal, if the LLM had served live?

Answer. (a) 2\times3\times10^9\times50=3\times10^{11}. (b) 3\times10^{11}\times300\times5{,}000=4.5\times10^{17} FLOPs per second. (c) Labeling: 10^8\times3\times10^{11}=3\times10^{19} FLOPs, equal to 3\times10^{19}/4.5\times10^{17}\approx67 seconds of live LLM serving. The student then serves at 10^7\times300\times5{,}000=1.5\times10^{13} FLOPs per second, 30,000 times less than the LLM.

Check: the one-time labeling job costs as much as about 67 seconds of live LLM ranking, which would otherwise run continuously. That asymmetry is why distillation is the dominant production pattern.
