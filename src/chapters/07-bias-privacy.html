Cross-cutting (2002–2025): feedback loops, causality and the privacy shock

In this chapter

  • Explain why a recommender trained on its own logs learns its own biases, and name the main ones (position, exposure, selection, popularity).
  • Correct position bias with inverse propensity weighting, and evaluate a new policy from old logs with IPS and doubly robust estimators.
  • Write Chapelle's delayed-feedback likelihood for conversions.
  • Explain why attributed conversions overstate what ads cause, and what randomized lift tests measure instead.
  • Date the privacy shocks (ITP, GDPR, ATT, Privacy Sandbox) and say what each removed from the models' inputs.

The gist. Every log a recommender learns from was produced by an earlier version of the same recommender, so the data record what was shown, where, and to whom, not what people would have chosen. Clicks on the top slot reflect the slot as much as the item; items never shown never get labels; ads get credit for purchases that would have happened anyway. From about 2016 the field borrowed tools from causal inference: propensity weighting, off-policy estimators and randomized holdouts. Then, from 2017, browsers, regulators and Apple removed much of the cross-site and cross-app identity these models consumed, forcing ad systems to learn from aggregated, delayed and noisier signals.

The data are generated by the policy

Logged interactions are outcomes of the deployed policy's choices, so naive training on them confounds item quality with exposure. Four biases recur:

  • Position bias. Users click higher positions more regardless of relevance.
  • Exposure (selection) bias. Labels exist only for shown items; for ratings, users choose what to rate, so missing ratings are not missing at random (Schnabel et al., ICML 2016, treated recommendations as treatments and corrected with propensities).
  • Popularity bias and feedback loops. Popular items are shown more, collect more positive labels, and are shown even more. Simulations by Chaney, Stewart and Engelhardt (RecSys 2018) show this algorithmic confounding homogenizes what users see without improving utility.
  • Conversion selection bias. Conversions are observed only after clicks (addressed by ESMM in chapter 5).

Position bias and inverse propensity weighting

Under the examination hypothesis, a click requires that the user examines the position and finds the item relevant; dividing each click by the examination probability of its position removes position bias in expectation. Write P(\text{click}\mid i,k)=\theta_k\cdot\gamma_i, where \theta_k is the probability that position k is examined and \gamma_i the probability that item i is clicked when examined. Joachims (KDD 2002) first trained rankers from click logs; Craswell et al. (WSDM 2008) proposed the cascade model, in which users scan down and stop at the first satisfying result; Joachims, Swaminathan and Schnabel (WSDM 2017) gave the propensity-weighted, unbiased learning-to-rank estimator.

Worked example

Two items, two positions

Given: examination probabilities \theta_1=1.0, \theta_3=0.4. Item A was shown 1,000 times at position 1 and got 100 clicks; item B was shown 1,000 times at position 3 and got 40 clicks.

  1. Naive CTR: A =0.10, B =0.04. A looks 2.5 times better.
  2. IPS-weighted clicks: A =100/1.0=100; B =40/0.4=100.
  3. Estimated relevance \hat\gamma: A =100/1{,}000=0.10, B =100/1{,}000=0.10.

Result: after correction the two items are equally relevant; the naive model would have kept B at position 3 for ever.

Check: \mathbb E[\text{clicks}_B]=1{,}000\,\theta_3\gamma_B=1{,}000\times0.4\times0.10=40, matching the data.

The propensities must themselves be estimated: by randomizing positions for a small slice of traffic (costly), by swapping adjacent results, or by an EM algorithm over regular logs (Wang et al., WSDM 2018). Deep rankers often take a different route: feed position as an input to a separate shallow “bias tower” during training and drop it at serving (YouTube, Zhao et al., RecSys 2019; Huawei's PAL, Guo et al., RecSys 2019), so the main network learns \gamma while the bias tower absorbs \theta.

Off-policy evaluation and learning

Logs from a randomized (or at least stochastic) policy let you estimate how a different policy would have performed, by reweighting each logged reward by the ratio of the two policies' action probabilities. If the logging policy \mu chose action a_i in context x_i and observed reward r_i, the inverse-propensity-score estimate of a new policy \pi's value is

\hat V_{\text{IPS}}(\pi)=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n r_i\,\frac{\pi(a_i\mid x_i)}{\mu(a_i\mid x_i)},

unbiased whenever \mu(a\mid x)>0 wherever \pi(a\mid x)>0. Its variance explodes when the policies differ, so practice clips the weights (trading bias for variance) or uses the doubly robust estimator (Dudík, Langford and Li, ICML 2011), which adds a reward model \hat r and uses IPS only to correct the model's residual:

\hat V_{\text{DR}}(\pi)=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\Bigl[\sum_a\pi(a\mid x_i)\,\hat r(x_i,a)+\frac{\pi(a_i\mid x_i)}{\mu(a_i\mid x_i)}\bigl(r_i-\hat r(x_i,a_i)\bigr)\Bigr].

It is unbiased if either the propensities or the reward model is correct. Bottou et al. (JMLR 2013) applied counterfactual reasoning to Bing's ad placement, estimating the effect of changes to the auction and the ad-load policy from logs with randomized perturbations. Swaminathan and Joachims (ICML 2015) turned evaluation into learning (counterfactual risk minimization, with a variance penalty).

YouTube's REINFORCE recommender (Chen et al., WSDM 2019) brought off-policy correction to a production policy-gradient system. The gradient of the policy's expected reward is reweighted by \pi_\theta(a\mid s)/\beta(a\mid s), with \beta the logging policy, itself estimated by a separate head because the logs mixed many earlier systems. Because the system recommends K items at once, the paper derives a top-K correction that multiplies the gradient by

\lambda_K(s,a)=K\bigl(1-\pi_\theta(a\mid s)\bigr)^{K-1},

which shrinks the push on items the policy already ranks with high probability (they will appear in the top K anyway) and boosts it for items with small probability.

The objective keeps moving: clicks, watch time, satisfaction

Each engagement proxy was adopted to fix the previous one's failure and then showed its own. Click optimization rewarded misleading thumbnails; YouTube began weighting recommendations by time watched in March 2012 and extended this to search that October. Watch time rewards long, compulsive viewing; platforms added survey-based satisfaction labels and “valued” engagement as additional ranking targets. Facebook announced a re-weighting toward “meaningful social interactions” among friends and family in January 2018. In ads, the analogous move went from clicks to conversions to conversion value (chapter 6). The common structure is a multi-task model whose heads are combined by a weighting formula, the place where product policy is set; the algorithms of chapter 5 do not choose the weights.

Delayed feedback: conversions arrive late

Conversions can arrive days after the click, so a recent unconverted click is not yet a negative; Chapelle (KDD 2014) modelled the conversion and its delay jointly. Let p(x) be the probability of eventual conversion and assume the delay D given conversion is exponential with rate \lambda(x). For a click observed for elapsed time e:

\begin{aligned}P(\text{converted at delay }d\mid x)&=p(x)\,\lambda(x)\,e^{-\lambda(x)d},\\ P(\text{no conversion yet}\mid x,e)&=1-p(x)+p(x)\,e^{-\lambda(x)e}.\end{aligned}

The second line is the key: a click that has waited only an hour contributes little evidence against conversion, one that has waited thirty days contributes almost full evidence. Training on “converted so far” labels would bias p(x) downward, most strongly for new campaigns, exactly the ones whose bids depend on it. Later work replaced the exponential assumption with non-parametric delay models and importance weighting for streaming training.

Attribution versus incrementality

Attribution credits an ad for conversions that follow it; incrementality is the number of conversions the ad caused, and only randomized experiments measure it reliably. Lewis and Rao (QJE 2015) showed that ad effects are so small relative to the variance of individual purchases that even very large experiments have little power, the “unfavorable economics” of measuring returns. Johnson, Lewis and Nubbemeyer (JMR 2017) introduced ghost ads: log, in the control group, the ads that would have been shown, so treatment and control are compared on the comparable subset and the experiment is far more precise. Gordon et al. (Marketing Science 2019) ran 15 large Facebook experiments and found that observational methods, even with rich covariates, often failed to recover the experimental lift, frequently overstating it. For model builders this means the label the ad system optimizes (attributed conversion) is not the quantity the advertiser should pay for (incremental conversion), a gap that uplift models, which rank by predicted treatment effect rather than predicted outcome, try to close.

The privacy shock, 2017–2025

Between 2017 and 2021 browsers, regulators and Apple removed most cross-site and cross-app user identifiers from advertising, and the industry's response changed what ad models learn from.

  1. Sep 2017
    Safari Intelligent Tracking Prevention (Safari 11) restricts third-party cookies with a classifier for tracking domains; Firefox enabled Enhanced Tracking Protection by default in 2019.
  2. Mar 2018
    SKAdNetwork arrives in iOS 11.3: Apple-mediated, aggregated install attribution with no user-level identifier. Later versions add a small conversion value, delayed postbacks and, in SKAdNetwork 4 (2022), multiple postbacks with crowd-size thresholds.
  3. May 2018
    GDPR becomes enforceable in the EU: consent and lawful-basis rules for personal data, including advertising identifiers. California's CCPA follows in January 2020.
  4. Aug 2019
    Google announces the Privacy Sandbox for Chrome: replace third-party cookies with on-device APIs (FLoC, later Topics; FLEDGE, later Protected Audience; Attribution Reporting with noise).
  5. Apr 2021
    App Tracking Transparency enforced from iOS 14.5: apps must ask permission to track users across other companies' apps and sites; the advertising identifier (IDFA) is zeroed unless the user opts in.
  6. Feb 2022
    Meta tells investors ATT will cost it about $10 billion of 2022 revenue Call.
  7. Jul 2024
    Google abandons third-party cookie deprecation in Chrome in favour of user choice; in April 2025 it drops even a standalone choice prompt.
  8. Oct 2025
    Google retires most Privacy Sandbox technologies, including Topics, Protected Audience and Attribution Reporting, citing low adoption; a few (CHIPS, FedCM, Private State Tokens) remain.

What changed in the models. On iOS, an ad network lost the user-level label linking an ad impression in one app to an install or purchase in another. Responses, roughly in order of adoption:

  • Server-side and first-party signals. Advertisers send conversions from their own servers (Meta's Conversions API, Google's enhanced conversions), matched on hashed first-party identifiers such as e-mail where users consented.
  • Modelled conversions. Platforms estimate the conversions they can no longer observe and report them alongside observed ones; the reported number becomes partly a model output.
  • Learning from aggregated labels. Training from postbacks that report counts per campaign with delay and noise, rather than per-impression labels; calibration at the aggregate level replaces per-user validation.
  • Contextual and on-platform signals. More weight on what the platform observes itself: in-app behaviour, the content being viewed, device and context features.

Inference. The privacy shock rewarded scale of first-party data and model capacity: platforms that observe both the ad exposure and the conversion inside their own surfaces (a marketplace, a closed app ecosystem, a game network with its own measurement partner) lost less signal, and the general advertising models of chapter 8 were partly a response to sparser, noisier labels. The Privacy Sandbox's retirement in 2025 left Chrome with third-party cookies still available and no standard privacy-preserving replacement in place.
